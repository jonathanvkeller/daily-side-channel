Today's question: **when does repetition stop making copies and start making something new?**

**No major signal today.** Yesterday produced a serious contender for your next loud listening session, but that is a discovery, not a breaking-news emergency.

## LISTENING ROOM · the riff is not going to rescue you

Austin's **Water Damage** released *Repair* on **October 23** via 12XU: four performances, each roughly nineteen minutes long, spread across two LPs.

The band describes its approach as **MAXIMAL REPETITION / MINIMAL DEVIATION**. It is not an empty slogan. Rather than playing a riff until the chorus arrives, the ensemble lets the riff become the environment. You begin hearing the tiny collisions inside it: cymbals, viola, feedback, the slight disagreement between players.

The core group has ten members; *Repair* adds guitar from Wendy Eisenberg and electronics or bass from Ricardo Dias Gomes. The track names mostly identify recording reels rather than trying to supply a story. "Reel 43 Down On The Street" openly points at the Stooges.

Start with **"Reel 45."** It comes out swinging, then refuses the expected release for more than nineteen minutes. Stuart Berman's October 23 review makes the apt comparison to hearing repeated studio takes of *Fun House*: exposure changes the listener as much as the performance.

**The interesting event isn't the next chord. It's the moment your ears stop treating the same chord as the same thing.**

[Listen to *Repair* on the band's Bandcamp](https://waterdamage12xu.bandcamp.com/album/repair). [Read the October 23 review](https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/water-damage-repair-review).

## FOUND OBJECT · the solo RPG that uses the postal service as a mechanic

**The Machine**, by Adira Slattery and Fen Slattery, begins with a person compelled to build an impossible device. The machine calls to them. They document the obsession in a small notebook, using playing cards to drive the journal.

Then comes the design decision that makes this different from another solitary writing exercise: **the notebook is meant to be mailed to the next player**.

Only one person plays at a time. The next player inherits the physical record and continues the cycle with another character. A single person can also play successive characters alone; the creators explicitly support that interpretation.

The game is not about optimizing a machine until it works. Its creators say recurring failure and downfall were central to the design. The notebook becomes a history of people who thought they might be the exception.

This is a particularly strange kind of multiplayer: no shared session, no synchronized schedules, just a physical artifact carrying an obsession from one owner to another.

The game costs $5 and contains dark themes including obsession, self-harm, and death. It is worth reading the content notes before playing.

[Explore *The Machine* and its rules description](https://adira.itch.io/the-machine). The creators also discuss solo succession and the intended failure loop in the [game's comments](https://adira.itch.io/the-machine/comments).

## STUDIO FUEL · archive the scene through what it made for itself

On **October 19**, Letterform Archive published an interview with graffiti writer and type designer Kel Troughton about building a collection of approximately **700 graffiti books, zines, and pieces of ephemera**.

The crucial distinction is that the collection is not primarily a gallery of polished paintings. It includes magazines circulated among writers, small-run books, sketches, and posters documenting freight-train pieces. Some items survived because somebody kept what was never intended to be permanent.

One contributor brought annual posters celebrating a crew's painted freight cars. Another set of donated magazines contained a deceased writer's drawings on ordinary lined paper. Those small scraps are evidence of a community speaking to itself, not a retrospective written for outsiders.

**Steal the archival principle, not somebody else's visual style:** when inventing a fictional music scene or tabletop setting, create the things its participants would actually pass around. A rehearsal-room notice. A set list with corrections. A flyer with the wrong address. A photocopied apology.

Those artifacts can imply an entire social world without an omniscient narrator explaining it.

[Read the Letterform Archive interview and examine the zines](https://letterformarchive.org/news/an-undercover-look-at-letterform-archives-graffiti-collection/).

## RABBIT HOLE · the television that bends the picture before it reaches the screen

In the early 1970s, **Nam June Paik** and **Shuya Abe** developed a device called the **Wobbulator**. It is a modified cathode-ray-tube television whose picture can be warped into waves, loops, and unstable abstractions.

The trick isn't an effect applied to a video file. Extra hardware produces changing magnetic fields that deflect the electron beam inside the television. The signal may be ordinary; the *apparatus displaying it* has been persuaded to misbehave.

The object has an excellent survival story. A former CalArts student, William McGuigan, rescued an original Wobbulator after it had been deemed unsafe and slated for disposal. He later built a working replica. Both were delivered to the Smithsonian American Art Museum in **2024**, allowing the museum to preserve the original and demonstrate the effect without operating the fragile historic device.

It is a useful reminder that an artwork can reside partly in a process, not just in the image left behind.

Do not try to reproduce this by opening an old CRT television: the high-voltage components can be dangerous even when unplugged. The interesting thing to borrow is the *idea of altering the display process*.

[See the original, the replica, and the explanation at the Smithsonian American Art Museum](https://americanart.si.edu/blog/nam-june-paik-wobbulator).

## CROSSWIRE · the same input, a different machine

Put Water Damage beside the Wobbulator.

One keeps a musical pattern running until small differences become enormous. The other keeps a video signal running while the display hardware bends what you see.

In both cases, the creative material is not simply a sequence of new events. It is **a stable input subjected to an unstable process**.

Now apply that to a solo RPG. A settlement broadcasts the same four-note warning every night. Each neighborhood hears it through a different damaged receiver. One hears a lullaby; another hears a summons; a third hears the missing fifth note.

The players aren't investigating which broadcast was sent. They're investigating the machines, bodies, and traditions that transformed it.

**Design principle: change the channel of transmission before changing the message.** The resulting contradictions can generate a mystery without needing a secret villain or a stack of prewritten lore.

## 20 MINUTES · record a machine slowly going wrong

Pick one repeatable sound: an open guitar string, a bass note, a pencil tapping a mug, or a muted chord.

Record **ninety seconds** of it on your phone. Keep the rhythm as steady as you comfortably can. Do not deliberately introduce a big change.

Listen once for what stayed constant. Listen again for the tiny accidents: a missed hit, a new overtone, a change in attack, a background sound that briefly takes over.

On an index card, write a fictional maintenance report with three timestamps. Treat those accidents as evidence that the sound came from a machine with a purpose.

For example: *00:42 — the motor begins to imitate a second operator.*

End with one sentence explaining what the machine was built to do, and why its owner refuses to switch it off.

No track to finish. No new game to design. Just one recording and a small, troubling document.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A riff becomes a landscape through repetition. A notebook becomes a game by traveling between hands. A zine preserves a scene's private language. A television creates art by misdirecting the machinery that displays an image.

**Sometimes you don't need new material. You need a more interesting way for the old material to survive the trip.**

And sometimes the machine isn't broken. It has simply developed an opinion about what you keep feeding it.
