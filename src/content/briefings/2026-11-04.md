# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
*November 4, 2026 · No. 036*

## THE SIGNAL · a century of drawing sound

Daphne Oram would have turned 100 this year. On November 3, *The Quietus* published [a centenary oral-history feature](https://thequietus.com/news/daphne-oram-at-100-bradford-influence-hannah-peel-annea-lockwood/) gathering nine artists' reflections on her influence as celebrations culminate in Bradford.

Oram helped establish the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, but the more provocative legacy is **Oramics**: a system in which drawn marks on film could control electronic sound. Rather than record a performance and edit it, she worked toward *drawing instructions for what a sound should become*. This isn't a new gadget announcement. It's a reminder that electronic music's history contains alternative interfaces we still haven't exhausted.

The question worth stealing: what would you compose differently if the interface were a pencil instead of a keyboard?

## FOUND OBJECT · Britain's concrete ears

At Greatstone on England's southeast coast stand three immense acoustic mirrors, including a curved wall roughly 200 feet long. Built between the wars, these structures were designed to concentrate the distant sound of approaching aircraft onto listening equipment. They are monuments to a moment when **architecture itself was a sensor**.

The mirrors became obsolete as aircraft and detection technology changed, but they remain wonderfully alien objects: gigantic pieces of concrete whose job was to listen rather than shelter. They make a good companion to Oram's work because both treat sound as something that can be shaped by a physical interface before anyone interprets it.

[See the Greatstone sound mirrors](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/greatstone-sound-mirrors).

## STUDIO FUEL · lose the guitar inside the signal chain

In his [November 3 review of *Phren*](https://thequietus.com/quietus-reviews/marta-salogni-stefano-pilia-phren-review/), Daryl Worthington describes Marta Salogni and Stefano Pilia feeding sounds through filters and tape echo, then into Salogni's Revox reel-to-reel for further transformation. Pilia's guitar is often the starting input, but the finished sound resists being identified as guitar.

The stealable mechanism is **serial misidentification**. Take one recognizable source, pass it through two or three distinct transformations, and preserve just enough of its attack or rhythm to leave a faint trail back to the original. With a guitar, try a short scrape or harmonic, a delay, and a deliberately degraded recording. Don't aim for a preset called *haunted factory*. Listen for the moment the source becomes uncertain.

A useful constraint: keep the original recording, but don't let yourself add any new notes after the first pass. Everything else must be extracted from, delayed, cut, or layered out of that initial gesture.

## RABBIT HOLE · a hand is an index

Medieval and early modern memory systems sometimes mapped information onto the fingers and joints of a drawn or actual hand. The *Guidonian hand* became a way to teach and locate musical pitches; other mnemonic hands organized religious meditations and textual material.

A finger isn't just a symbol. It's an address that you can touch, point to, and revisit without opening a notebook. The hand becomes a tiny spatial interface, with its own physical hierarchy.

[The Public Domain Review's illustrated essay on mnemonic hands](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/handy-mnemonics/) includes examples from a 1491 devotional book, among many stranger variants.

The rabbit hole here is **embodied indexing**: information can be organized around a familiar shape instead of alphabet, chronology, or subject. A commonplace book could use a drawn hand as its contents page. The thumb might lead to unfinished ideas; the knuckles to contradictions; the fingertips to things worth trying.

## CROSSWIRE · a listening station that remembers with its fingers

Combine the concrete acoustic mirror with the mnemonic hand.

Imagine a fictional listening station whose operator records strange transmissions by marking positions on a hand diagram. The position tells you *where the sound was heard*, the ink shape tells you *what it resembled*, and a crossed-out mark means the station later heard a contradiction.

Over time, the operator's hand-chart becomes a map of events nobody can locate geographically. The archive isn't a transcript. It's a body-shaped index to an invisible landscape.

For a solo RPG, this creates an oracle with continuity: each new sound must connect to one existing mark, and each contradiction changes how an older mark is interpreted. You don't need a table of a hundred spooky noises. You need five locations and a reason for the same location to mean something different tomorrow.

## 20 MINUTES · draw a receiver

1. **Five minutes:** Draw the outline of your non-dominant hand. Give each fingertip a kind of sound: warning, invitation, machinery, animal, and impossible.
2. **Five minutes:** Listen to the room or a short instrumental track. Write one sound or imagined sound at each fingertip. No need to record audio.
3. **Five minutes:** Connect two fingertips with a line. Write a fictional explanation for why those sounds share an origin.
4. **Five minutes:** Cross out one label. Write a single sentence explaining what the station operator now believes instead.

Stop. The result is a weird little playable artifact, not a productivity system demanding maintenance.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

Oram drew sound into existence. Concrete walls once listened to the horizon. Tape can make a guitar forget its own name. A hand can become an address book for memory.

**An interface doesn't merely transmit information. It decides what can be noticed.**
