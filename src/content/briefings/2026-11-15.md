# Daily Side-Channel — November 15, 2026

*The Souvenir That Proves You Were There*

A Chicago beatmaker turns a label into a support system. A medieval pilgrim pins a tiny metal souvenir to their clothing. And a fictional scene discovers that its admission ticket can become its most dangerous artifact.

**Today's question: what happens when proof of participation becomes a way to control participation?**

## THE SIGNAL — a label as a local utility

**No major signal today.** The best recent find is smaller, and more useful: in Bandcamp Daily's **November 13, 2026** Essential Releases, Chicago producer **Radius Etc** appears with *The Time Is Coming…(There It Is)*. The record moves among abstract boom-bap, dub, jazz and ambient textures, but the story worth following is **ETC Records**, the label Radius founded in 2013 to support underground artists in Chicago and beyond.

That means the record isn't just a product of a scene. It's one output of an infrastructure that helps other artists release theirs. The beats can wander because the work around them is comparatively concrete: put music into circulation, connect people, keep the thing going.

**Listening route:** start with “Head Banger (BOOM!)” for the blunt end, then “Kama’Āina Kalimba & Ocean” to see how far the palette stretches.

## FOUND OBJECT — the medieval gig pin

The Met holds a fifteenth-century **pilgrim's badge depicting Saint Adrian**, made in the southern Netherlands around 1460–80. Adrian appears in armor holding a sword and an anvil. The badge is a little lead-alloy object, roughly eight centimeters high.

These weren't singular luxury commissions. Medieval pilgrimage sites sold badges and souvenirs, and the trade helped finance religious establishments. Badges could show that someone had visited a particular destination. In Adrian's case, the anvil also evokes his martyrdom and his association with blacksmiths.

There's something unexpectedly modern about the physical economy: travel somewhere, participate in a communal ritual, buy or receive a recognizable symbol, wear it where other people can see it. Not quite a band patch, not quite a festival wristband, but close enough to make the comparison interesting.

The object is especially good because it holds **a place, an event, an identity, and an economic transaction** in one tiny casting.

## STUDIO FUEL — make the artifact carry the network

Borrow the badge's information density, not its imagery. Invent a small physical token for a fictional music scene, trade guild, pilgrimage, or underground settlement. It must encode three things without explanatory text: **where it came from, what happened there, and what its owner is allowed to do next**.

Try designing it with only silhouette, one repeated symbol, and one deliberate imperfection. That last mark might identify the workshop, the production batch, or a specific event. Suddenly the object can function as memorabilia, authentication, and a clue.

For a real zine or notebook cover, the same constraint works as a design exercise: one icon that communicates a whole invented institution, without a paragraph of lore.

## RABBIT HOLE — proof is not the same as truth

The Met's medieval-art teaching materials describe pilgrimage tokens as evidence that a journey had been completed. That creates a delicious distinction: **the badge is portable; the experience it supposedly certifies is not**.

An object can be copied, sold, lost, inherited, stolen, or forged. Its meaning depends on the community that recognizes it and the procedure by which it was issued. This isn't a claim that every medieval badge was forged. It's a general problem with material credentials.

A badge might begin as a memory of a place. Over time, people may start treating the badge itself as more important than the journey.

## CROSSWIRE — the scene that issues its own passports

Put ETC Records beside the medieval pilgrim badge.

Imagine a noise-music scene where every basement venue casts a small metal token for each show. Collecting tokens gives you access to rehearsal spaces, recording equipment, or a label's communal pressing fund. The scene's physical archive becomes its membership system.

Then someone discovers a batch of tokens from a show that never happened.

You have several playable possibilities without choosing the correct one in advance: an old venue has been erased from memory; someone forged credentials to gain access; or the scene's official history was edited to exclude an entire group of people.

**The mechanic:** every token grants one real privilege and introduces one contested claim about the past. A collection is simultaneously inventory, social network, and unreliable history.

## 20 MINUTES — counterfeit a scene

Take one sheet of paper and draw **three gig badges** for an imaginary underground venue. Give each a year, an emblem, and a visible production flaw.

Now choose one badge and write a four-line description of the show it commemorates. For the second, write a short note from somebody who insists the show was canceled. For the third, write the rule that allows its bearer into a locked rehearsal room.

Finally, place all three badges in the pocket of someone who claims they've never heard of the venue.

Stop there. You've made a small artifact set with a built-in contradiction and an obvious reason for somebody to investigate.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A label keeps music circulating. A badge lets a traveler carry proof of an experience. Both depend on a community agreeing that the object, and the institution behind it, mean something.

**The weird part isn't that a token remembers where you've been. It's that it may decide where you're allowed to go next.**
