# Daily Side-Channel — November 22, 2026

*The Instrument That Hasn't Been Built*

A record label records musicians inside prisons. An eighteenth-century catalog documents instruments that don't yet exist. A solo RPG gives you a language to invent, and a finite number of turns before its last speaker dies.

Today's question: **does documenting something help it survive, or merely describe its disappearance?**

## THE SIGNAL — the record label that has to bring the studio with it

On November 20, Bandcamp Daily published [FREER Records and the Art of Liberation](https://daily.bandcamp.com/features/freer-records-label-profile), a detailed account of a label making music with currently and formerly incarcerated artists.

According to the feature, its team has recorded in 17 prisons across 10 states, working with 65 incarcerated and 18 formerly incarcerated musicians. Recording sessions can require years of permissions, correspondence, logistical work, and negotiation over artistic control. The label insists on uncensored work and fights to secure royalties and publishing for its artists.

One fascinating example is *No Moon*, pairing incarcerated poet Spoon Jackson's phone-recorded voice with compositions by Nicolas Snyder. Another is Territorial's *TLAXIHUIQUI*, whose seven musicians made an improbable mixture of blues, hip-hop, country, and Nahuatl chant.

The consequential point isn't simply that these recordings exist. **The label is a production infrastructure designed around people whom the conventional music business cannot easily reach.** A studio is normally somewhere you go. Here, access itself becomes the largest part of the recording process.

## FOUND OBJECT — an encyclopedia of instruments that might exist someday

In 1722, Filippo Buonanni published *Gabinetto Armonico*, an illustrated catalog of musical instruments inspired by Athanasius Kircher's Roman collection. Its 152 engraved plates, by Arnold van Westerhout, include familiar instruments, obscure sound-making devices, incidental noises, and proposed machines that had not actually been built.

The [Public Domain Review's illustrated selection](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/filippo-buonanni-harmonic-cabinet/) includes an especially splendid category problem: a beekeeper striking a tub belongs in the same catalog as elaborate organs and imaginary acoustic machinery.

The book classifies instruments by strings, winds, and percussion, but also discusses music according to its social purpose: funerals, warfare, sacrifice, and seafaring.

**A catalog of instruments can also be a catalog of the situations in which people need sound.**

Better still, it doesn't require every instrument to exist before granting it an entry.

## STUDIO FUEL — write the catalog entry before inventing the sound

Design an imaginary instrument using four fields: **excitation** (what sets it vibrating), **control** (what the player can change), **failure** (what it does unpredictably), and **occasion** (when society uses it).

For example: *The Mourning Comb*. Excitation: dragging wet cord across ceramic teeth. Control: tension and angle. Failure: it occasionally produces a second tone after the performer stops. Occasion: announcing the closure of a workshop whose owner died without an apprentice.

Now translate the imagined mechanism into guitar gestures or household noises. You aren't trying to build the original object faithfully. You're trying to find a physical performance that makes its description believable.

The occasion is crucial. An instrument with a social job has a reason to exist beyond sounding cool.

## RABBIT HOLE — the dictionary that dies with its author

[Runique](https://erysrpg.itch.io/runique), a free solo/GMless game by Erys, asks you to create a writing system and fragments of a language for the last surviving member of a people.

A tarot deck supplies strokes for the script, then words and prompts that gradually build a vocabulary and the character's experiences. When the deck runs out, the character dies and their culture ends.

That's a severe and elegant design decision. The deck is simultaneously a creative oracle and a lifespan.

Unlike a conventional constructed-language exercise, the objective isn't to finish a grammar. You are making something that is guaranteed to remain incomplete.

**A dictionary usually promises that words can be retrieved later. This one makes every definition feel like time borrowed from an ending.**

The game's premise and mechanics here are drawn from its creator's description, not a completed playthrough.

## CROSSWIRE — a museum of sounds nobody can perform anymore

Combine Buonanni's speculative instruments with *Runique*'s terminal language.

Imagine a museum that preserves meticulous descriptions of instruments, but not their players. Every exhibit specifies its construction, sound, and ritual use. Some are real; some are proposals; some are deliberate mistranslations.

Visitors can rebuild an instrument, but that doesn't establish how it was played.

Make each reconstruction answer one question while destroying another: the restored mechanism clarifies its tuning but erases evidence of wear; a surviving song explains its social purpose but uses words no living speaker understands.

This creates a useful solo-RPG oracle: whenever you reconstruct a cultural artifact, **gain one technical fact and lose one contextual certainty**.

Not every discovery makes the past more legible.

## 20 MINUTES — invent the last instrument

1. **Five minutes:** Sketch an instrument using three household materials. Name its excitation, control, and failure.
2. **Five minutes:** Write a short museum label explaining the social occasion on which it was played.
3. **Five minutes:** Invent three words in its culture's language: one for the performer, one for the occasion, and one with no direct English equivalent.
4. **Five minutes:** Write a correction in the museum catalog, dated a century later. The correction must make the exhibit stranger, not more complete.

Stop there. One artifact, three words, two incompatible interpretations. That's enough of a civilization to leave a mark.

---

**Today's residue:** A record label needs infrastructure to preserve voices. An encyclopedia preserves instruments that never existed. A game lets you invent a language while counting down to its extinction.

**Preservation isn't the opposite of loss. Sometimes it's the shape loss leaves behind.**
