# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**November 5, 2026 · No. 037**

*The Map Is Lying About the Cheese*

A metal scene that survives because its members build the shows themselves. A piece of cheese becomes a monster movie. And a cartographer learns that putting something on a map is not the same as understanding it.

Today's question: **who gets to decide what counts as the territory?**

## THE SIGNAL · the underground is its own infrastructure

**No major signal today.** But J. Bennett's [November 3 report on Nepal's extreme-metal underground](https://daily.bandcamp.com/scene-report/nepal-metal-scene-report) is worth reading for the infrastructure, not just the riffs.

Kathmandu's scene, according to musician Darshan Angdembe Limbu of Animality, is largely organized by bands, friends, and participants rather than an established industry. Death metal, grind, thrash, and hardcore often share bills rather than maintaining isolated sub-scenes. Earlier outdoor shows gradually gave way to indoor venues; Purple Haze and Reggae Bar became important spaces with stages and sound equipment.

The article supplies a listening route through Animality, Discord, Nude Terror, Shree 3, and others. **Start with Shree 3 if you want desert-rock heft; try Discord if you want the grindcore end of the ecosystem.**

The story is not that a scene somehow exists despite lacking an industry. The scene *is* the network of people doing the work.

## FOUND OBJECT · the cheese is alive, unfortunately

In [*The Cheese Mites* (1903)](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/the-cheese-mites-1903/), Charles Urban and Francis Martin Duncan turn a meal into a small horror show. A diner examines his cheese through a magnifying glass, and the film cuts to a microscopic view of mites crawling over one another.

The startling effect is a change in scale, not an invented monster. The film belonged to the *Unseen World* series and demonstrated that scientific microscopy could be popular entertainment.

**The familiar object remains physically unchanged. The viewer's relationship to it does not.** A piece of cheese becomes an inhabited landscape as soon as the camera chooses a different scale.

## STUDIO FUEL · make the map one hex at a time

[Lilith's *TRAILMARK*](https://lilithsrpgs.itch.io/trailmark) is a solo cartography RPG organized around twelve expeditions. Each outing adds one hex to a hand-drawn map. Terrain, discoveries, hazards, rumors, supplies, and the cost of crossing give the growing picture consequences. The designer deliberately provides no illustrated landscape: the map that matters is the one you make.

The stealable mechanism is **one new place per session, with a cost attached to reaching it**. Don't generate a whole continent and then try to care about it. Draw one hex, name it, and record what the crossing took from the traveler. The next hex inherits that history.

The game's own page discloses AI-assisted text; the useful observation here is about its published rules and physical play structure, not an endorsement of its production process.

## RABBIT HOLE · California was an island for a surprisingly long time

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century maps repeatedly depicted [California as an island](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/maps-showing-california-as-an-island/). The Public Domain Review gathers examples from Stanford's collection, including a map by Joan Vinckeboons from around 1650.

The error had roots in early exploration and geographical reporting, but its endurance is the fascinating part. A map's apparent precision can give a mistaken claim the authority of something already established. Later mapmakers can inherit the mistake along with the coastlines, lettering, and decorative confidence.

**A beautifully drawn map can be a highly efficient way of preserving an error.**

The rabbit hole is not merely mistaken geography. It's how copied representations acquire authority, and how difficult it can become to distinguish observation from inheritance.

## CROSSWIRE · the cartographer and the microscope disagree

Put *TRAILMARK* beside *The Cheese Mites*, then let island California enter the room.

A cartographer draws a region one expedition at a time. A microscope reveals an entire world inside something the map calls merely *food*. An old atlas confidently records a coastline that doesn't exist.

Imagine a solo exploration game with **three incompatible scales of truth**:

- The official atlas records what the kingdom believes.
- The expedition map records what the traveler can verify.
- The microscopic map records what inhabits the objects everyone overlooks.

Every time the traveler updates one map, they must choose whether to correct the others. That correction may threaten a trade route, a sacred boundary, or the reputation of someone who has been teaching the old map for generations.

The mechanism: **discoveries don't just add information; they create obligations to revise previous claims.**

## 20 MINUTES · survey a false island

Take one sheet of paper and draw a small island with three landmarks: a tower, a quarry, and a village.

**First five minutes:** Write an official map legend claiming the island is uninhabited beyond the village.

**Next five:** Add one observation from a traveler that contradicts the legend without proving it entirely wrong.

**Next five:** Pick an ordinary object from the village and imagine it magnified a thousand times. Draw one feature that makes it look like terrain.

**Final five:** Write a cartographer's note explaining which map they will publish and which fact they will leave out.

Stop there. You have three competing accounts of one place and a person responsible for choosing between them.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A music scene can be its own infrastructure. A microscope can turn lunch into an alien landscape. A solo game can make every new hex matter because reaching it costs something. A centuries-old map can look authoritative while remaining profoundly wrong.

**Representation isn't possession of the truth. It's a decision about which part of the truth to make visible.**
