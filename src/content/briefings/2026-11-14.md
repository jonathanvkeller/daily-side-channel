# Daily Side-Channel — November 14, 2026
*The Radio Station Is a Living Index*

A radio station turns fifty without becoming a museum. A dead office worker leaves eight objects to classify. And a seven-bridge puzzle asks whether the problem is the route, or the demand that you never repeat yourself.

## THE SIGNAL — a radio station that grows record labels

**No major signal today.** The most consequential recent piece in this orbit is Jimmy Trash's [November 11 account of 3RRR's fiftieth anniversary](https://daily.bandcamp.com/features/3rrr-50th-anniversary-feature).

Melbourne's community station began in 1976. Its anniversary series, *50 Years in 50 Days*, reconstructs its history through interviews, archival performances, and programs. But the important thing isn't longevity. Presenters have gone on to run labels including Chapter Music, Efficient Space, and CEASEANDDESIST; broadcasts lead to gigs, gigs lead to records, and records lead back to broadcasts.

Michael Kucyk describes how digging through the station's vinyl and CD library helped inspire the *Midnite Spares* compilation. Its packaging even borrowed the look of a station library sticker.

**The station isn't merely covering a scene. It's one of the mechanisms by which the scene reproduces itself.** The archive becomes a seed bank, not a mausoleum.

## FOUND OBJECT — the dead colleague's desk drawer

[The Last Inventory](https://oakhaven.itch.io/the-last-inventory), by Elias Sterling Oakhaven, is a one-page solo journaling game. You are a registrar in a Night Operations Division. A colleague has died at their desk. Before dawn, you must log eight objects from the drawer and decide what gets filed and what gets burned.

A deck of cards and about thirty minutes are the advertised requirements. The game page discloses AI-assisted text.

What's good about the premise is the refusal to make the protagonist a detective with unlimited freedom. You're a bureaucrat with a deadline and a narrow authority. **Classification itself is the moral action.** Whatever you call evidence may survive; whatever you call rubbish may vanish.

## STUDIO FUEL — program a radio show from one box

Borrow 3RRR's archive-to-label pipeline in miniature. Take six unrelated objects, tracks, or notebook scraps. Arrange them as a fifteen-minute imaginary radio show, but require each transition to explain a *relationship* rather than a genre.

A broken cassette leads to a field recording because both preserve accidental sound. A field recording leads to a recipe because both describe a place through what happens there. A recipe leads to a funeral notice because somebody kept cooking after its author died.

No ranking, no definitive taxonomy. **Sequencing is an argument that can be revised without changing the objects.** That's useful for mixtapes, zines, commonplace books, and solo-RPG discovery tables.

## RABBIT HOLE — the bridges that refuse your itinerary

In 1736, Leonhard Euler analyzed the famous seven bridges of Königsberg. Could someone cross every bridge exactly once on a single walk?

No. The remarkable insight is that you can discard almost everything about the city: distances, angles, architecture, even the shape of the river. Keep only landmasses as dots and bridges as connections.

For a walk using every bridge exactly once, at most two dots can have an odd number of bridges attached: one for the start and one for the finish. Königsberg's historical network had four odd-degree dots. Impossible.

This is an established foundational result in graph theory, not a new discovery. [MathWorld's explanation](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/KoenigsbergBridgeProblem.html) is a good entry point.

The surprising creative implication: **sometimes no amount of clever navigation solves a structurally impossible itinerary.** You must change a connection, relax a rule, or accept repetition.

## CROSSWIRE — the archive that cannot be completely visited

Combine the bridge problem with *The Last Inventory*.

Imagine a library where each room represents a kind of record, and each doorway represents a permitted transfer between classifications. The registrar must inspect every doorway once before sealing the building.

But the network has four odd-degree rooms. The prescribed inspection is mathematically impossible.

Now the story begins. Do you falsify a log, cross one doorway twice, or create an unauthorized passage? A procedural failure becomes a character decision.

A system can be internally consistent and still demand something impossible from the person operating it.

## 20 MINUTES — the impossible inspection

1. **Five minutes:** Draw four rooms and seven connections between them. Arrange them so all four rooms have an odd number of connections.
2. **Five minutes:** Try to trace every connection exactly once without lifting your pencil. Notice where you get stuck.
3. **Five minutes:** Give the rooms four bureaucratic names: Intake, Evidence, Disposal, and Appeals. Assign one suspicious object to each.
4. **Five minutes:** Invent a clerk who must certify that every passage was inspected exactly once. Write the sentence in their report that conceals what actually happened.

Stop. You've got a physical impossibility, a procedural rule, and an ethical compromise. No campaign bible necessary.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A radio archive makes new records possible. A desk drawer forces a choice about preservation. A bridge network refuses to cooperate with an elegant plan.

**An information system is never just a container. Its connections decide which journeys are possible, and which lies become tempting.**
