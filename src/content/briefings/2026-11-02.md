# Daily Side-Channel No. 034
**Monday, November 2, 2026**

*The Missing Record Is the Instrument*

Eight turntables with no records. A book of reviews of books that don't exist. And a theorem saying that, with enough folding, one straight cut can make almost any straight-edged drawing.

**Today's question: what happens when you remove the thing an apparatus was supposedly built to handle?**

## THE SIGNAL · no major signal today

**No major signal today.** Nothing in the latest sweep earned the emergency lighting. The recent record below is worth listening to because of how it was made, not because it dropped this morning.

## LISTENING ROOM · the turntables have been relieved of their records

**Institut für Feinmotorik — *Öd*** (Faitiche, **October 9, 2026**).

The German collective has a wonderfully unreasonable instrument: the **Octogrammoticum**, built from eight prepared turntables, four mixers, and ordinary objects such as rubber bands, paper scraps, foil, and CDs. The crucial inventory item is **zero records**. The rotating decks are no longer playback devices; they're motors for friction, contact, and tiny rhythmic accidents.

The group has worked with prepared turntables since the late 1990s. On *Öd*, six pieces were recorded live on the apparatus in Berlin-Marzahn in 2024. The resulting music occupies a narrow corridor between minimal techno, noise, and a drawer of household junk quietly declaring independence.

The title is a German word suggesting bleakness or desolation. The track titles, credited to poet Dagmara Kraus, are another small oddity: language that feels close to intelligible without settling down.

Start with **“ruder also in den”** or **“bald rose sags fast.”** Listen for pulses produced by objects physically interfering with machinery, rather than a beat assembled from conventional drum sounds.

The point isn't that turntables are obsolete. It's that the machine still has interesting abilities after its expected cargo is removed.

Sources: [Artist's Bandcamp and recording notes](https://institut-fuer-feinmotorik.bandcamp.com/album/d) · [Faitiche's 2026 catalog](https://faitiche.de/products/) · [The Quietus review, October 13](https://thequietus.com/quietus-reviews/institut-fur-feinmotorik-od/).

## FOUND OBJECT · a book of criticism with no books behind it

**Stanisław Lem — *A Perfect Vacuum*** (originally published in Polish in **1971**).

Lem wrote reviews of books that had never been written, including books that might be impossible to write. One of the reviews even turns on *A Perfect Vacuum* itself. It isn't simply a set of imaginary jacket blurbs: the supposed criticism becomes the entire literary work.

The fake books provide room for philosophical experiments, parody, literary feuds, and speculative premises that don't have to survive 300 pages of conventional plot. Lem can argue about an invented novel's failures without first having to produce the novel.

There's a delicious structural reversal here. Usually the book exists and criticism follows. Lem makes criticism the source from which the reader reconstructs an absent book.

This is not a newly discovered manuscript, and the imaginary titles aren't real publications. The actual book is very real, and Michael Kandel's English translation is available in a Northwestern University Press edition.

Sources: [Lem's official site](https://english.lem.pl/works/apocryphs/a-perfect-vacuum/113-a-perfect-vacuum) · [MIT Press Bookstore edition details](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780810117334) · [The New Yorker's discussion of Lem's imaginary reviews](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/culture-desk/the-beautiful-mind-bending-of-stanislaw-lem).

## STUDIO FUEL · write the failure before the work

Borrow the **imaginary review** as a constraint, not Lem's prose.

Instead of designing a fictional book, game, or record, begin with a critical sentence about something that does not exist:

> The game's best mechanic is also its worst: every successful negotiation erases one of the parties' reasons for meeting.

That's an invented example, not a quotation from Lem.

Now ask what rules would make the sentence accurate. What counts as success? How does a reason disappear? What happens after enough victories?

The review forces you to design *consequences* before you design lore. Better still, it can reveal that the imaginary work would be more interesting as a short story, a solo RPG, or a deliberately unplayable artifact.

Don't build the whole thing. Keep the most productive contradiction.

## RABBIT HOLE · one straight cut can make an absurdly complicated shape

Suppose you draw a five-pointed star on paper. Could you fold the sheet flat and cut out that exact outline with **one straight scissor cut**?

Yes. Now make the drawing more complicated: a polygon with holes, several separate polygons, even an arrangement of straight segments.

The **fold-and-cut theorem** says that, in an idealized mathematical setting with sufficiently elaborate flat folds, *any finite straight-line cut pattern* can be produced by a single complete straight cut through the folded sheet.

This is an established theorem, not a new preprint or an unproved conjecture. Erik Demaine, Martin Demaine, and collaborators developed proofs and algorithms around the problem. An early published example appears in a Japanese mathematical puzzle book from **1721**.

The intuitive trick is to stop imagining scissors tracing an outline. Instead, imagine folding the paper until **all the edges you want cut line up along one straight line**. The cut is trivial. The folding is the hard part.

It's not a claim that any ordinary handful of folds will work, or that thick real-world paper can always realize the mathematical construction. The theorem concerns idealized paper and sufficiently sophisticated crease patterns.

The researcher's site has examples to print and try, which is much better than merely staring at the claim and deciding mathematics has become unreasonable.

Sources: [Erik Demaine's fold-and-cut project, with examples](https://erikdemaine.org/foldcut/) · [Original research paper](https://martindemaine.org/papers/JCDCG98/).

## CROSSWIRE · build the response before the source

The recordless turntables and Lem's imaginary reviews are performing opposite versions of the same operation.

**Institut für Feinmotorik keeps the playback apparatus but removes the record. Lem keeps the apparatus of criticism but removes the book.**

In both cases, what seemed like a secondary process becomes capable of producing the primary work.

For worldbuilding or solo RPG design, try building an institution through its *responses* before inventing what it responds to. Write a maintenance warning for a machine nobody has described. Draft a hostile review of a festival that hasn't been designed. Invent an insurance exclusion for an event your setting's inhabitants refuse to explain.

Then reverse-engineer only enough of the missing source to make the response coherent.

This is not just faux archival dressing. The institution's reaction establishes what it fears, values, measures, or considers normal. You learn about a society through its habits of interpretation.

The fold-and-cut theorem adds a useful caution: the visible result can be simple while the hidden preparation is extraordinarily intricate. Don't confuse the single cut with the whole process.

## 20 MINUTES · review a game that nobody has made

**Paper, pen, and a mildly unfair critical voice.**

**Minutes 0–5:** Give a nonexistent solo RPG a title and write its one-star review headline. It must identify a *specific mechanical failure*, not just say the game is boring.

**Minutes 5–10:** Write 100–150 words from the perspective of someone who played it. Include one concrete scene, one surprising rule, and one thing the reviewer grudgingly admired.

**Minutes 10–15:** Extract the rule implied by that criticism. Write it in no more than three sentences, as though it belonged in the game's actual instructions.

**Minutes 15–20:** Test the rule against a single imaginary decision. If it produces an interesting consequence, keep it. If not, revise the criticism instead of adding more rules.

Stop with one review and one rule. No product roadmap. No invented Kickstarter. The bad review has already done enough damage.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A turntable can make music without playing a record. A review can become literature without reviewing a book. A complicated drawing can become a single straight cut if the folds do enough work first.

**The supposedly secondary operation might be the entire art.**
