# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**October 28, 2026 · No. 029**

*A music player descended from devotional technology, a book whose pages are silk, and a solo RPG that refuses to give you an answer until you go outside.*

Today's question: **what if the medium isn't where you put the work, but what the work actually does?**

## THE SIGNAL · no major signal today

**No major signal today.** The worthwhile recent discovery is a deeply reported history of an ambient-music object that deliberately does almost nothing. That's not an emergency, and it doesn't need to pretend to be.

## LISTENING ROOM · the little box that refuses to become a phone

In an **October 26** Bandcamp Daily feature, Mike McGrath-Bryan traces the history of **FM3's Buddha Machine** through an interview with co-creator Christiaan Virant.

The familiar version is a small box with a speaker, a volume control, and a selection of short ambient loops. No playlists, no skip recommendations, no elaborate interface. It plays the sound that's in it until you intervene.

The less familiar version is where it came from. Virant recalls hearing recorded chanting at a temple in China, eventually discovering a repurposed answering-machine tape loop. Later he encountered dedicated Buddhist chanting devices, *niànfó jī*, which helped inspire the FM3 project. These were existing devotional technologies; FM3 adapted the form for experimental music rather than inventing the underlying idea.

Virant and Zhang Jian developed the first FM3 version in the mid-2000s, negotiating a small manufacturing run and loading the box with nine loops. The interview gets satisfyingly specific about the economics of making an odd physical object before cheap, flexible manufacturing was a given.

**The interesting constraint isn't the small number of sounds. It's the refusal to give the listener a thousand ways to escape them.** The box isn't a streaming service with a worse screen. It's a different proposition about listening.

Source: [Bandcamp Daily, "How the Buddha Machine Built Modern Ambient" (October 26, 2026)](https://daily.bandcamp.com/features/the-buddha-machine-feature).

## FOUND OBJECT · a book made on a loom

The **1886–87 *Livre de Prières*** from Lyon looks like a lavish revival of an illuminated medieval prayer book. But its leaves aren't ink on parchment or paper.

**They're woven silk.**

The text and images were produced using Jacquard looms, with designs encoded through hundreds of thousands of punched cards. The resulting fabric leaves were mounted on paper and bound into a book. It took a technology associated with industrial textile production and made it impersonate the painstaking handwork of medieval manuscripts.

The project drew on medieval and Renaissance visual models while also advertising Lyon's nineteenth-century technical skill. Scholars estimate a single edition of roughly **50–60 copies**.

There's something wonderfully excessive about this. The makers could have printed a book about the history of weaving. Instead, they made the *book itself* a demonstration of what weaving could do.

Open the scans and zoom into the letterforms. Your brain keeps insisting you're looking at print.

Source: [The Public Domain Review, "Programming Prayer: The Woven Book of Hours (1886–87)"](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/lyon-woven-prayer-book/), drawing on material from the Walters Art Museum.

## STUDIO FUEL · photograph the mess before it becomes design

Graphic designer **Martin Venezky** makes compositions from physical materials: fragments of type, photographs, paper, clear tape, and layered arrangements that occupy an awkward territory between two and three dimensions.

Letterform Archive's **September 21** interview describes the process behind his work and an exhibition of more than fifty process images, on view through **November 8, 2026**. Some collages are assembled by hand and then scanned or photographed; the final design can be a small part of a much larger trail of experiments.

What I would steal isn't the recognizable aesthetic. It's the **change of medium in the middle of the process**.

Arrange scraps on a table. Photograph them. The camera turns a layered, temporary construction into a flat image, introducing cropping, shadows, focus, and unexpected relationships. Move the pieces and do it again.

The photograph isn't merely documentation. It becomes another tool for investigating the materials.

Venezky's position is unusually resistant to the idea that a process should be made efficient. The experiments are not detours around the work. They *are* the work.

Source: [Letterform Archive, "Martin Venezky: Process Work" (September 21, 2026)](https://letterformarchive.org/news/martin-venezky-process-work/).

## RABBIT HOLE · an oracle that requires shoes

**The Oracle's Stroll**, by **Scrap Yarn Games**, is a one-page solo walking RPG. You play a village oracle whose neighbors keep showing up with questions. Rather than consulting a giant table of invented symbols, you take an actual walk and interpret what you notice outside.

The game uses a **d10**, pen and paper, ten-minute intervals, and a leisurely **30-minute walk**. It costs $5, with community copies offered on its itch.io page.

The important distinction is between *randomly receiving an answer* and *paying attention until something becomes an answer*.

A broken branch, an unexpectedly loud bird, a fence repaired with the wrong material: none of these are inherently omens. The game gives them a job by placing a fictional question in your mind before you encounter them.

That's an elegant solo-RPG design move. Instead of asking an oracle to fabricate more fictional content, you let the physical environment supply surprises the designer couldn't have written in advance.

Source: [Scrap Yarn Games, "The Oracle's Stroll"](https://scrap-yarn-games.itch.io/the-oracles-stroll). The full game is designed around a 30-minute walk; the exercise below is an independent miniature, not a substitute for its rules.

## CROSSWIRE · the camera is the oracle's second pair of eyes

Put Venezky's photographed collages beside *The Oracle's Stroll*.

One process turns ordinary materials into a composition by changing their arrangement and viewpoint. The other turns ordinary observations into possible answers by changing the question asked of them.

Combine them and you get a peculiar form of fieldwork: **ask a fictional question, collect visual evidence, and assemble the answer without inventing any new objects.**

Suppose your solo character asks why the village well was sealed. You walk around the block and notice a cracked painted line, a discarded glove, and a tree growing through a fence.

Photograph or sketch them. Arrange the three observations on a page. Now interpret the *relationships* between them rather than assigning each a fixed symbolic meaning.

Maybe the glove belongs to someone who tried to repair the boundary. Maybe the crack is the route something took. Maybe the tree is evidence that the seal is older than anyone admits.

You haven't discovered the truth about a real well. You've made a fictional answer from stubbornly real materials.

**The useful collision: let the world choose your vocabulary, then let composition choose the grammar.**

## 20 MINUTES · assemble an answer you didn't plan

Write this question at the top of a page: **"Why did the last keeper abandon the station?"**

Spend **ten minutes** walking somewhere safe, or looking carefully around your home if going outside isn't practical. Find three unrelated details: a shape, a damaged surface, and something that seems out of place. Sketch them or take photos.

Spend **five minutes** arranging those three observations on paper. No additional drawings. Change only their positions, scale, or order.

Spend the final **five minutes** writing the keeper's last log entry. It must mention all three details, but it must not explain everything.

Stop. You've made a field report from evidence that wasn't waiting for you to arrive.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A tiny box turns a handful of loops into a different way of listening. A loom becomes a book-making machine. A pile of scraps becomes a composition when the camera intervenes. A walk becomes an oracle when somebody brings a question.

**A medium is not neutral packaging. It changes what the work is capable of asking.**
