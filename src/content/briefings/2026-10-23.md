# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**October 23, 2026 · No. 024**

*A music scene built around a disused fuel tank, Victorian creatures shaken out of ink, and a mathematical tile that can make a surprisingly convincing record sleeve.*

Today's question: **how much can you make from a small rule and an accident?**

## THE SIGNAL · a fuel tank develops an export route

On **October 22**, *The Quietus* published an interview with Gonçalo F. Cardoso about an unusual collaboration: Tenerife's experimental-music festival **Keroxen** and the DIY label **Discrepant** are bringing three nights of music to London's **Cafe OTO**, October 29–31.

The detail that makes this more than another festival announcement is the infrastructure. Keroxen's home is **El Tanque**, a former fuel-storage tank in Santa Cruz de Tenerife repurposed for experimental performance. Cardoso describes the island's underground as unusually close-knit, but says finding physical places to perform locally is still difficult.

The London bill includes Canarian psych-rock band GAF y La Estrella de la Muerte, guitarist Paul Pèrrim, turntablist DJ Sniff, and experimental musician Ale Hop. Cafe OTO also confirms a record market, a light installation, and a free vinyl record with each individual admission.

The interesting thing isn't that a small scene has been invited to a larger city. **A venue, a festival, and a label have built a route for one another's artists and records.** That is what scene infrastructure looks like when it works: not an algorithm discovering an audience, but people creating a place for the music to happen.

## FOUND OBJECT · the goblins that fell out of an ink bottle

In 1896, Ruth McEnery Stuart and Albert Bigelow Paine published **_Gobolinks, or Shadow-Pictures for Young and Old_**, a book of creatures made from folded inkblots, accompanied by comic verse.

Its inhabitants include **the Kangar-Rooster-Roo** and **a Flit-Flit Flitter**. Some appear to have acquired far more tails than any reasonable animal needs.

The process reverses conventional illustration. You don't decide what a creature looks like and then draw it. You make a stain, fold the paper to mirror it, and discover what sort of creature the accident has produced. Only afterward do you name it and invent its behavior.

The book belongs to a larger nineteenth-century inkblot tradition. Physician-poet Justinus Kerner made far darker symmetrical figures, while later books pushed the technique toward games and nonsense poetry. This was imaginative play long before inkblots became popularly associated with psychological testing.

**The object is a bestiary whose animals were found rather than designed.** And because the original is digitized, you can examine the actual creatures instead of reading somebody's tidy description of them.

[Read the 1896 book at the Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/16017793/) · [Explore the inkblot-book family tree at The Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/inkblot-books/)

## STUDIO FUEL · eight pages, one sheet, one cut

A small tool released on **October 1** deserves a look: [**10 minute zine**](https://10minutezine.com/), made by illustrator and musician Daniil Vlasenko.

It's a free browser-based editor for the classic eight-page mini-zine folded from one sheet of paper. You add text and images, export a print-ready PDF, then make three folds and one cut. It supports US Letter as well as A4; the creator says there's no account, no ads, and no upload of your zine to a server.

The useful part isn't the promise of speed. It's that the **physical page count is fixed before you start**.

Eight pages are enough for a cover, a premise, four discoveries, an instruction, and an ending. They are not enough for a sprawling lore encyclopedia. Good. That forces the object to have a shape.

Steal the constraint even if you never open the editor: decide what each of eight panels *does* before writing what it *says*. A miniature horror game might use one panel for the rule, four for locations, two for consequences, and one for the final question.

The editor handles the upside-down imposition so the paper can remain the interesting problem.

[Try the free zine maker](https://10minutezine.com/) · [See its October 1 release notes](https://3apb.itch.io/10-minute-zine)

## RABBIT HOLE · two curves, sixty-five thousand possibilities

In 1704, Sébastien Truchet studied patterns made by rotating simple triangular designs inside square tiles. A later variation, often called a **Truchet tile**, replaces the diagonals with **two quarter-circle arcs**.

Every square has just two possible orientations.

Put sixteen squares in a 4 × 4 grid, and there are **2¹⁶ = 65,536** possible orientation arrangements before accounting for rotations or reflections. No elaborate algorithm is required. Flip a coin for each tile.

What appears is unexpectedly organic: looping corridors, interrupted ribbons, isolated circuits, and lines that wander off the page. You made sixteen binary decisions; the eye sees a network.

This is established recreational mathematics, not a new theorem. The interesting idea is **emergence from local rules**: no tile knows the overall pattern, but every tile changes what its neighbors can connect to.

A little grid of arcs can resemble a circuit diagram, an abstract city, an album cover, or a parasite under a microscope. It's one of those rare mathematical toys that is understandable in seconds and still worth staring at afterward.

[See the tiles and examples at Wolfram MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/TruchetTiling.html)

## CROSSWIRE · a Victorian monster joins a noise-rock label

Put _Gobolinks_ beside Truchet tiling.

The inkblot produces **a shape without a plan**. The tiles produce **a pattern without a designer choosing the whole pattern**. One relies on the eye finding a creature; the other relies on tiny geometric decisions accumulating into a system.

Together they suggest a visual language for an imaginary DIY record label.

Every release gets one folded blot, its accidental mascot. Every release also gets a grid of two-arc tiles, generated by coin flips. The blot supplies the personality; the grid supplies a recognizable family resemblance.

The covers would share a procedure without looking identical. You could make a dozen before deciding what the label's imaginary bands sound like.

**That's the useful collision: establish an identity through a repeatable method, not a rigid template.** The output can remain surprising without losing its connection to the rest of the series.

And because the ingredients are paper, ink, and simple geometry, it could survive photocopying beautifully.

## 20 MINUTES · manufacture a record that doesn't exist

Make a fake seven-inch sleeve. No computer required.

1. **Five minutes:** put a drop of ink or diluted paint on scrap paper, fold it, press, and unfold. If you don't want wet materials, scribble a dark shape and mirror it by hand. Give the resulting creature an undeservedly dignified name.
2. **Seven minutes:** draw a 4 × 4 grid. In each square, draw the same pair of quarter-circle arcs, choosing one of the two orientations with a coin flip. Trace one resulting path with a heavier pen.
3. **Eight minutes:** put the creature and the grid together as a black-and-white record sleeve. Invent the band name, two track titles, and a tiny catalog number. One track must be named after something you genuinely see in the blot; the other after something the pattern suggests.

Stop at twenty minutes. Don't design the entire fictional label. Don't start pricing risograph printing.

You've made one impossible artifact. That's enough.
