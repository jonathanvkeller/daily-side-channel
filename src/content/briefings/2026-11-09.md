# Daily Side-Channel — November 9, 2026

*The Picture Was Added Thirty Years Later*

A silent occult film gets a second life in harsh noise and cello. A calligrapher's showpiece receives a swarm of insects thirty years after he wrote it. And a book hides a painting that appears only when its pages are bent.

Today's question: **can a second artist change a work without erasing the first?**

## THE SIGNAL — no major signal today

No major signal today. The worthwhile recent release is a new reissue of an older experiment, not an urgent development.

## LISTENING ROOM — an occult film gets a different nervous system

On November 6, Bandcamp Daily highlighted **EARTHSLAYER's _HÄXAN_**, a remixed and remastered reissue of the Dublin experimental group's 2016 rescore of Benjamin Christensen's 1922 silent film _Häxan_. Their palette runs from harsh noise and cello to unsettling vocals, horns, ambient passages and doom-heavy weight. The reissue is available on cassette.

The attraction is not a claim that the new soundtrack is the definitive meaning of the film. A silent film is unusually open to being reframed by its accompaniment. Change the score and a sequence can tilt from ethnographic curiosity toward dread, or from spectacle toward grief.

**Borrowable question:** what happens when a soundtrack refuses to tell the viewer what emotion to feel?

Source: [Bandcamp Daily, Essential Releases, November 6, 2026](https://daily.bandcamp.com/essential-releases/essential-releases-november-6-2026).

## FOUND OBJECT — a collaboration separated by thirty years

The Getty's **_Mira calligraphiae monumenta_** began as Georg Bocskay's model book of virtuoso handwriting, written in 1561–62. About thirty years later, Emperor Rudolf II commissioned Joris Hoefnagel to illuminate it. Hoefnagel filled the spaces around the elaborate scripts with meticulously painted insects, fruit, flowers, shells and other small organisms.

The original calligrapher had died before the second artist began. The illustrations don't merely decorate the writing; they make the blank space compete with it. A demonstration of handwriting becomes a miniature ecosystem.

The Getty has detailed images and a discussion of the pigments and astonishingly fine brushwork.

Sources: [Getty collection record](https://www.getty.edu/art/collection/object/103RWD); [Getty's close reading of the manuscript](https://www.getty.edu/news/to-hold-nature-in-the-hand-revealing-the-wonders-of-the-mira-monumenta-calligraphiae/).

## STUDIO FUEL — add a second voice without revising the first

Try **delayed collaboration with yourself**. Make a page of six lines, a rough guitar motif, or a tiny drawing. Put it aside. When you return, the original is untouchable. Your only permitted additions are in the gaps: marginal marks, silence, counter-rhythm, framing, or interruptions.

This is a constraint, not a historical claim about how Hoefnagel worked. The point is to make the second pass answer the first rather than polish it. Revision asks, 'How do I fix this?' Counterpoint asks, 'What else is happening here?'

For guitar: record a thirty-second riff, then make a second track using only feedback, scrapes, dead notes and the spaces between attacks. No doubling the riff. No new chord progression.

## RABBIT HOLE — the book that hides its picture until you bend it

A **disappearing fore-edge painting** is painted onto the outer edges of a book's pages while the text block is fanned at an angle. Close the book and the image vanishes; the exposed edge may even be gilded to conceal it. Fan the pages again and the image returns.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes a 1796 edition of medieval French tales at The Cloisters whose fanned edge reveals a knight riding toward a woman. The book's physical manipulation becomes the viewing mechanism. It's an illustration that doesn't live on a page.

This is not a magical ink trick. The angled stack exposes slightly different strips of paper than the closed edge.

Sources: [The Met's account](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/perspectives/fore-edge); [Boston Public Library examples via The Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/fore-edge-book-paintings-from-the-boston-public-library/).

## CROSSWIRE — make the second layer conditional

Connect the film rescore, Hoefnagel's insects, and the fore-edge painting.

Each is a work with an additional interpretive layer, but the conditions differ. A soundtrack changes what a viewer feels *over time*. Marginal illumination changes where the eye wanders *across a page*. A fore-edge painting appears only when the object is *handled differently*.

A useful design idea emerges: **don't hide the secret behind a successful perception roll; make the player perform a different operation on the artifact**.

In a solo RPG, a ledger reveals one story when read normally, another when its pages are fanned, and a third when entries are read aloud to a particular rhythm. The clues aren't three random secrets. They're three modes of encountering the same object.

## 20 MINUTES — the hostile accompaniment

1. **Five minutes:** Write four plain sentences describing an ordinary place. A bus stop, kitchen, or rehearsal room. Keep the language observational.
2. **Five minutes:** Without changing those sentences, add four marginal annotations by an imagined stranger who believes the place is dangerous. Each annotation must refer to a real detail in the original.
3. **Five minutes:** Add a third layer: one instruction for how the page must be physically handled (turned, folded, read upside down, held to light) to reveal a different relationship among the details. You can simulate it with a folded scrap of paper.
4. **Five minutes:** Read the original alone, then with the annotations. Decide which single detail changed meaning most. Stop.

No elaborate lore. The object now has three ways of being read.
