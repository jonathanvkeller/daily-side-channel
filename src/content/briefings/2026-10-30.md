# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**October 30, 2026 · No. 031**

*The Dead Have an Address Book*

A folk ensemble lets old songs change their minds, a medieval obituary goes on tour with a courier who doesn't survive the job, and archaeology offers a way to build mysteries from things that cannot all have happened at once.

Today's question: **what if the past is not a timeline, but a set of relationships you have to reconstruct?**

**No major signal today.** But *The Quietus* published its October music selections this morning, October 30, and one record is worth following into the weeds.

## LISTENING ROOM · a hunting song mourns the fox

London's nine-piece **Shovel Dance Collective** released *Everything in Readiness* on October 23 through tak:til. The record uses traditional songs, field recordings, cassette manipulation, and an ensemble whose instruments include fiddle, banjo, dulcimer, bass clarinet, harp, and pump organ.

The most revealing entry point is **"Tally Ho! Hark Away!"** The collective took a fox-hunting tune and changed its emotional allegiance. Instead of celebrating the hunters, their version mourns the fox. Horns and cymbals become something closer to a funeral procession or a strange ascension.

This isn't merely a louder arrangement of an old song. **The music retains its inherited structure while changing who the listener is asked to stand beside.**

The October 30 *Quietus* roundup calls attention to the record's movement between old and new, fact and invention. The band's own notes describe the work as a patchwork assembled from increasingly specific, underperformed traditional material.

Start with "Tally Ho! Hark Away!" and then "Who Killed Cock Robin?" Listen for the moment an old tune stops feeling like an antique and starts sounding like an argument happening now.

## FOUND OBJECT · the obituary that outlived its messenger

Around **1455**, the Premonstratensian abbey at West Dereham in Norfolk produced a parchment **mortuary roll** to announce the death of Abbot John Wygenhale and request prayers from other religious houses.

This was not a document mailed to one recipient and filed away. A *rotulifer*, or roll-bearer, carried it between monasteries, which could add written responses. The surviving British Library roll is about **1.32 metres long**, with a decorated heading, an illustrated burial scene, and evidence of visits to multiple religious houses.

Here's the detail that stops the machinery: **the roll records two successive couriers because the first, Johannes Foster, died during the mission.** Nicholaus Scrop continued the work.

And the document kept going after its original purpose ended. Names and dates were erased and replaced so it could commemorate later abbots. The old death notice became a reusable vehicle for new deaths.

The roll is simultaneously a memorial, a route through a social network, and a palimpsest of institutional memory. Its physical alterations tell a story its official text wasn't trying to tell.

## STUDIO FUEL · let centuries pass between turns

**The Skeletons**, from Bully Pulpit Games, turns the familiar dungeon crawl around. Instead of playing adventurers entering a tomb, you play the cursed skeletal guardians who have to defend it.

The game supports **one to six players**. Long stretches of darkness and silence separate the intrusions. The tomb changes; the guardians change; and flashes of conflict gradually recover pieces of their forgotten lives.

It's a $10 digital game, with character sheets, map-drawing, and a short rules PDF.

The mechanism worth borrowing is not "play as a skeleton." It's **give the player very little agency over the passage of time, then give them one intense moment in which to act**.

A conventional campaign asks what you do tomorrow. This structure asks what survives you after a hundred years, and what you recognize when you're briefly awake again.

For solo play, try ending a scene immediately after a consequential action, advancing the world by an unknown number of years, and beginning the next scene with evidence of what that action became.

## RABBIT HOLE · archaeology as a logic puzzle

In **1973**, archaeologist Edward C. Harris developed the **Harris matrix**, a way to represent the relative order of deposits, cuts, and other archaeological contexts.

Imagine a stone floor. Someone digs a grave through it. Later, someone fills the grave. You can establish a sequence without knowing a single calendar date:

**floor laid → grave cut → grave filled**

Now add a painted symbol on a nearby wall. You know it was painted after the wall was built, but nothing yet tells you whether it predates the grave. A careful diagram leaves that relationship **unknown**.

That's the important bit. A Harris matrix isn't a complete timeline with guesses filling every gap. It's a diagram of what the physical evidence actually constrains. Two events can remain unordered.

Archaeologists use it to reason about sites whose surviving layers are far more complicated than a neat stack of centuries. Cuts can interrupt older deposits, and later activity can remove earlier evidence.

For a worldbuilder or mystery designer, this is gold: **build a sequence of facts that must be true, and leave the rest unresolved until play supplies evidence.** That's a more interesting mystery than hiding a predetermined answer behind an arbitrary roll.

## CROSSWIRE · the tomb's only history is a traveling document

Combine the West Dereham mortuary roll with *The Skeletons*.

Imagine a tomb whose guardians wake only when intruders enter. Outside, a memorial scroll travels between settlements. Every visitor adds a name, a prayer, a correction, or an accusation.

The player doesn't receive a chronological campaign journal. They receive the scroll **as it exists at the next awakening**.

Some entries have been scraped away. One village has changed its name. Two witnesses disagree about who was buried. A courier's handwriting stops halfway through a sentence, and another hand finishes the route.

The tomb itself supplies a second record: broken seals, newer masonry, graves cut through older floors.

Now you have two imperfect histories, one written and one physical. Use the logic of a Harris matrix to identify what must have happened before what. Let the contradictions remain contradictions.

**The game isn't about discovering a secret lore document. The documents are the changing world, and the player is reconstructing its history from damage.**

## 20 MINUTES · excavate a place that doesn't exist

Draw a small cross-section of a tomb, not a map from above. Give it a floor, a sealed doorway, and one later intrusion.

Write four observations on separate scraps:

- The original floor is intact beneath the sealed doorway.
- A tunnel cuts through that floor.
- Loose stones fill the tunnel.
- A painted warning appears on a wall, but its age is uncertain.

Spend five minutes drawing the **known** before-and-after relationships. Don't assign a date to the painting.

Spend five minutes inventing a fifth piece of evidence that changes one relationship without making the evidence logically impossible.

Spend the final ten writing a three-sentence field note by an excavator who has realized the tomb was opened for a reason other than theft.

Stop. The unknown date is not a hole to patch. It's a place for a future session to begin.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

An old hunting tune changes sides. A death notice travels farther than its first courier. A skeleton remembers in bursts separated by centuries. A diagram refuses to invent dates it cannot prove.

**The past doesn't arrive in order. We impose order on the pieces that survive, and sometimes the most interesting answer is that we still don't know.**
