## LISTENING ROOM · Pardoner discovers subtraction

San Francisco's Pardoner released [*Target Practice*](https://pardoner.bandcamp.com/album/target-practice) on **October 16**, through Bar/None Records.

Eleven tracks. About twenty-three minutes. Fewer abrupt changes, fewer surprise blast beats, more direct guitar songs that still sound suitably unkempt.

The band's stated change of direction is interesting because it isn't really a retreat from complexity. They're removing the stylistic detours that previously interrupted their songs.

Start with **“Don't Slouch”** for the compressed guitar attack and **“Twisted”** for the improbable sweetness beneath the distortion. The opening track, “Canary,” even credits a saxophone.

Jennifer Kelly's [October 16 review for Bandcamp Daily](https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/pardoner-target-practice-review) hears that tension between abrasive sound and pop melody.

**The useful lesson:** taking something away can make the remaining parts louder.

## FOUND OBJECT · an Italian board game built in Gujarat

The Metropolitan Museum of Art owns a **late-sixteenth-century chess-and-goose game board** made in Gujarat, India.

One side is for chess. The other is for the Game of the Goose, a dice-driven race along a spiral toward the center.

The board is constructed from ebony, ivory, horn, and gold wire. Its design follows an Italian Renaissance print, while the workmanship points toward Gujarati artisans producing luxury goods for European customers.

The Met suggests it may be the earliest surviving goose-game board.

The game itself is wonderfully indifferent to your tactical brilliance. Roll, move, encounter a helpful or disastrous space, repeat. Traditional boards have 63 spaces, including a notorious square that sends you back to the beginning.

Consider the journey this design made: an Italian printed game became a luxury object manufactured in India, and the same basic spiral format later appeared in inexpensive printed games elsewhere.

**A simple system of random movement became an extraordinarily portable piece of culture.**

For solo RPG design, that's practically an invitation. Replace the geese with shrines, the hazards with omens, and the center with a destination nobody is particularly enthusiastic about reaching.

[Examine the original board at The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/202612).

## STUDIO FUEL · give uncertainty its own die

[*Unfinished Business*](https://thaliathemuse.itch.io/unfinished-business) is a pay-what-you-want solo horror RPG released October 1 by Thalia.

The character returns to a significant place to confront a monster. The creator describes a flexible resolution system and a **Fear Die** intended to surprise the solo player, alongside random tables for inspiration.

It's an early version, currently labeled 0.1.1, rather than an established, thoroughly playtested system.

But the mechanism suggests something worth stealing: separate two questions that conventional RPG dice often collapse into one.

**Did the character accomplish the action?**

**What new uncertainty appeared because of it?**

You can successfully open a locked door while discovering something beyond it that changes your understanding of the entire building.

For solo play, that's particularly useful. You already know what you intended to do. The oracle's job isn't necessarily to stop you. Sometimes its job is to make your success unexpectedly complicated.

## RABBIT HOLE · the margins had their own illustrators

A **manicule** is a pointing hand drawn beside a passage a reader wants to remember.

It's essentially an analogue hyperlink, complete with an illustrated cursor.

But early readers didn't restrict themselves to tidy little index fingers.

In a July 2025 essay for the Folger Shakespeare Library, Erika Giddens examines annotations in a copy of Erasmus's *Adages*. The illustrations include a rooster manicule, a punk-rock manicule, and sword-fighting manicules.

The same book contains owls, rabbits, snakes, and other marginal creatures.

Another [Folger investigation of memory and marginalia](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/folger-deep-dives-memory-marginalia-and-the-art-of-reading-v-b-32-and-beyond/) documents readers adding faces, animals, and decorative brackets to early books. One particularly deranged pointing hand holds a branch that sprouts little human heads.

These weren't necessarily idle doodles. Readers used marks to navigate, remember, and respond to the text.

And sometimes the marks were more elaborate than the passage being marked.

**A book could acquire a second voice after it was printed: the reader's.**

That's a more interesting model for a commonplace notebook than inventing seventeen metadata fields and hoping the system somehow becomes meaningful.

The margin isn't merely space for notes. It's where the reader gets to argue back.

[See the illustrated manicules at Folger](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/adages-and-annotations/).

## CROSSWIRE · make a board game that remembers its players

Now combine the Game of the Goose with the manicule.

A conventional board stays the same between turns. You move through it, obeying what the spaces say.

But what if every time you revisited a square, you were required to **annotate it**?

The first time you encounter the bridge, it's simply a bridge.

The second time, you draw a hand pointing to it and write: *Nobody remembers crossing this.*

The third time, that annotation becomes a rule: *The next traveler must leave something behind.*

The board gradually acquires another author: the accumulated experience of the player.

Add the Fear Die idea and you have a small, portable horror engine. The die doesn't necessarily decide whether you can cross the bridge. It might decide what the bridge now means.

**The map stays put. The interpretation rots.**

That's a surprisingly complete game design principle: recurring locations, persistent annotations, and consequences that develop through revisiting rather than through a prewritten plot.

## 20 MINUTES · draw the road that remembers you

Take an index card or a notebook page. Draw a winding path of twelve numbered spaces leading to a house. Put a bridge on space 4, a shrine on 8, and a gate on 11.

Start at zero. Roll a d6, move forward, and write **one sensory detail** about wherever you land.

If you land on 4, 8, or 11, draw a little pointing hand in the margin and add a warning. Then return to the previous space you visited, or to zero if this was your first move.

Whenever you revisit a space, its original detail remains true, but you must add something that makes it less reassuring.

Stop when you reach or pass twelve, or after six rolls, whichever comes first.

Write one final sentence answering: **Who has been leaving the warnings?**

No character sheet. No campaign binder. No thirty-page rules document.

Just one small road with a memory.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A band removes its detours. A board game survives by being endlessly repurposed. A solo RPG makes uncertainty into a separate mechanism. Early readers turn book margins into a second conversation.

The recurring idea isn't merely that rules can be broken.

**Rules establish the journey. The marks left by people living with those rules are where the interesting story begins.**

Sometimes the most revealing part of an old book is the little hand pointing at something its owner couldn't bear to forget.
