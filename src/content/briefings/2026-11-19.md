# Daily Side-Channel
*November 19, 2026 · No. 051*

## THE SIGNAL — a magazine you could put in a Walkman

Yesterday, November 18, Bandcamp Daily published Erick Bradshaw's excellent history of **TELLUS**, the New York audio-cassette magazine launched in 1983 by Joseph Nechvatal, Carol Parkinson, and Claudia Gould. Earth Libraries is revisiting the series in 2026 with an anthology drawn from fourteen volumes.

TELLUS wasn't simply a mixtape with a fashionable masthead. It used a cheap, duplicable recording format to connect artists, composers, punk musicians, poets, and listeners who might never have entered the same gallery. College radio DJs played it; museums and libraries collected it. The underlying infrastructure mattered: Studio PASS and Harvestworks gave artists access to synthesizers, recording facilities, and cassette equipment.

My favorite detail: the 1990 *FluxTellus* package included two steel ball bearings. Shake the case and the packaging itself performs a sound piece by Takako Saito. The magazine was an instrument.

**The consequential idea:** a publication can distribute a scene's methods, not just report on its finished work.

## FOUND OBJECT — a roleplaying game with no pages

[**Foleynauts** by SeveralSeals](https://severalseals.itch.io/foleynauts) is a worldbuilding game delivered as an audio file. The premise: you've returned from a cave ecosystem where taking samples, recording, or illuminating the environment was forbidden. You must reconstruct what you heard and sensed using everyday objects.

The creator calls it a “no page” game, developed from the idea of a “one track” RPG. The page lists a downloadable MP3, optional friends, and optional recording software; the game remains in development.

It's an intriguing reversal of the usual solo-RPG habit. Instead of writing a description of an imaginary place, you make a physical approximation of its sounds. The resulting artifact is evidence of an expedition that never happened.

## STUDIO FUEL — make the packaging part of the composition

Steal one mechanism from *FluxTellus*: **the container must contribute information that the contents cannot.**

For a cassette or zine, a fold, rattle, perforation, or hidden surface could add a second channel. For a notebook, make the cover's texture or binding tell a story that the written pages never explicitly explain. For a guitar recording, let a physical sound, such as a case latch or an amp switch, become the final beat.

Constraint: don't decorate the package with a symbol representing the work. Make the package *do* something.

## RABBIT HOLE — fuzz that doesn't smother the song

On November 18, Bandcamp Daily also reviewed [Soup Activists' *Where the Rubbish Goes*](https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/soup-activists-where-the-rubbish-goes-review), the third album from the St. Louis project led by Martin Meyer, formerly of Lumpy and the Dumpers.

The review describes eleven tracks in roughly twenty-five minutes: quick rhythms, animated bass lines, melodic synth or guitar leads, and a gratifying layer of fuzz. The closer, “Hair 2000,” is just fifty-two seconds of receding noise.

There's a useful songwriting problem here: **how much distortion can you add before the melodic information disappears?** Not a question of whether the tone is heavy enough. Ask what the bass, voice, and lead each need to communicate, and let the dirt occupy the remaining space.

Try “Chateau Kids” and “Amnesia” for contrasting lead instruments; finish with “Hair 2000” to hear the record retreat from its own hooks.

## CROSSWIRE — a field guide to a place that cannot be photographed

Combine TELLUS's distributed audio magazine with *Foleynauts*' forbidden cave ecology.

Imagine a solo worldbuilding campaign where each discovered settlement produces one **audio issue** rather than a map entry. An issue contains three things: a local environmental sound, a fragment of speech, and an object that makes a noise when handled.

The player can circulate copies, but every copy loses or alters one layer. A settlement might recognize itself in the sound of its machinery but reject the spoken account. Another might claim that a recording attributed to its caves actually belongs to a place that no longer exists.

The important rule: **an archive doesn't just preserve a world. Circulating it changes the relationships inside that world.**

This could work without recording equipment. Write three onomatopoeic descriptions on index cards, then exchange, reorder, or deliberately misread them.

## 20 MINUTES — issue zero of a nonexistent audio magazine

1. **Five minutes:** choose a fictional place and write three sounds it produces. Avoid naming their sources.
2. **Five minutes:** reproduce the sounds with objects at hand. A paper bag, a mug, a zipper, and a tabletop are plenty.
3. **Five minutes:** arrange the three sounds as a tiny audio essay. Give it a title and write one sentence of liner notes that makes a questionable claim about the place.
4. **Five minutes:** invent a listener who hears the piece and identifies one sound incorrectly. Write what they now believe about the place.

Stop. No elaborate sound library, no full setting bible. One issue, one unreliable listener.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A magazine can be a cassette. Its case can be an instrument. A roleplaying game can be an audio track. And a noisy pop record can make its final statement by letting the song collapse into sound.

**Sometimes the medium isn't carrying the message. It's participating in the argument.**
