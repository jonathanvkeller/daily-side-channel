# Daily Side-Channel — November 6, 2026

*The Laboratory That Recorded Its Own Ghost*

An abandoned telecommunications laboratory makes a record of its own electromagnetic residue. A disembodied lich steals bodies to escape a dungeon. And a tiny experiment turns a room's ordinary machines into a fictional crime scene.

**Today's question:** what does a machine reveal when you stop asking it to perform its intended job?

## THE SIGNAL — no major signal today

**No major signal today.** But Matthew Blackwell's November 4 [Bandcamp Daily field-recordings survey](https://daily.bandcamp.com/best-field-recordings/the-best-field-recordings-on-bandcamp-october-2026) contains an unusually good piece of sonic archaeology. No need to pretend it's breaking news.

## FOUND OBJECT — the laboratory records its own ghost

**Rick Blything, *Beneath the Tower*.** An underground acoustic laboratory beneath the BT Tower at Adastral Park in Suffolk once supported telecommunications research. Its anechoic chamber and reverberation room were abandoned after their research role became obsolete in the late 1990s.

Blything gained access and made recordings using microphones sensitive to electromagnetic fields. He powered up old VHS players and measurement equipment, captured the otherwise inaudible electromagnetic activity, then stretched and looped it into four tracks.

That's a terrific reversal: a laboratory partly concerned with managing electromagnetic interference becomes the source of an album made *from* interference.

[Read about the album and listen via Bandcamp Daily](https://daily.bandcamp.com/best-field-recordings/the-best-field-recordings-on-bandcamp-october-2026).

## ANOTHER FREQUENCY — the animal that sounds like a synthesizer

**Anna McMichael and Helen Svoboda, *The Weddell Seal*.** Violin and double bass respond to underwater Weddell seal recordings made by Paul Cziko in Antarctica. The seals produce an astonishing range of whistles, chirps, pings, and science-fictional bleeps. The musicians don't merely decorate wildlife audio; they enter into a musical conversation with sounds that already have structure.

There's a useful humility here. The seal isn't an eccentric synthesizer preset. It's an animal communicating in a context we don't fully understand. The record invites musical response without pretending that response is translation.

[Find *The Weddell Seal* in the November 4 field-recordings column](https://daily.bandcamp.com/best-field-recordings/the-best-field-recordings-on-bandcamp-october-2026).

## STUDIO FUEL — let the body be disposable

**[Lichdome](https://toniokabonio.itch.io/lichdome)** by Anthony Grasso is a small solo RPG with a delightful mechanical insult to the conventional hero. You are a lich's severed head, imprisoned in catacombs. To escape, you resurrect corpses and use them as temporary bodies while climbing toward the Deadkeeper, who has your original corpse.

The game tracks stats and inventory with dice and can be played in about half an hour. Its interesting design move is separating **the continuing character** from **the replaceable body that makes action possible**.

Steal that separation. In a solo science-fiction game, perhaps your protagonist is a transmission inhabiting a series of failing robots. Each host offers one special ability and one limitation. Losing a host isn't automatically death; it is a change in what the protagonist can do, and in what they must abandon.

## RABBIT HOLE — a microphone for the sound that isn't sound

A conventional microphone detects pressure changes in air. An electromagnetic pickup detects changing magnetic fields and converts them into an electrical signal. Hold an appropriate induction pickup near a powered device and you may hear buzzing, pulses, and other electrical activity that were never meant to reach your ears.

This isn't a supernatural voice trapped in the circuit. It's a translation between physical phenomena: changing electromagnetic fields become an audio signal. The exact result depends on the pickup, the equipment, and its operating state.

That makes Blything's record particularly interesting. He's not simply recording a room's acoustics. He's making a second, invisible layer of the room available to listening.

For an approachable introduction, start with [Bandcamp Daily's account of *Beneath the Tower*](https://daily.bandcamp.com/best-field-recordings/the-best-field-recordings-on-bandcamp-october-2026).

## CROSSWIRE — the haunted machine is the character sheet

Put *Lichdome* beside *Beneath the Tower*.

Imagine a solo RPG in which the player is a consciousness that can occupy abandoned machines. A photocopier, tape deck, elevator controller, or laboratory instrument becomes a temporary body. Each has an intended purpose, a defect, and a residual signal.

The defect is a limitation. The intended purpose is an ability. The residual signal is a memory the machine cannot explain.

You don't need to invent a personality for every machine. **Let its physical function and failure history determine what it can perceive.** A tape recorder remembers sequences but cannot see. A security camera sees but has no hands. A vending machine can exchange objects but cannot travel.

The horror comes from trying to remain one person while your senses and available actions change every time you move.

## 20 MINUTES — the equipment room

Take a sheet of paper. No electronics required.

1. **Five minutes:** draw three abandoned machines in a locked room. Give each one a function, a defect, and one sound it might make.
2. **Five minutes:** invent a disembodied visitor who can inhabit only one machine at a time. Write one thing each machine can do that the others cannot.
3. **Five minutes:** put an important object just outside the room. Work out how the visitor could move it using only the machines' established functions. No new powers.
4. **Five minutes:** introduce one unexpected signal from a machine that is supposedly switched off. Write the first two sentences of the visitor's explanation, then stop.

You've built a tiny puzzle, a character constraint, and a mystery from three pieces of imaginary junk. Don't turn it into a 200-page systems manual.
