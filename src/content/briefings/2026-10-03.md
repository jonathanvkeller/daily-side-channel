## THE SIGNAL · a cave can apparently be a sentence

A new open-access *Antiquity* paper takes another look at three faded red glyphs in Cave 19 at Chalcatzingo, Mexico, and the interesting part is not merely that they appear to belong to the Teotihuacan writing system. The tiny rockshelter is halfway up a steep cliff. One glyph seems to name the place **Singing Mountain**. Another records ceremonial or martial regalia. A third frames a fire-blackened stone shelf and resembles structures used in Teotihuacan ceremonies commemorating the dead. The authors argue that sometime around the fourth to sixth centuries, writing, burning, landscape and ritual were operating there as one system.

So this isn't quite "someone wrote on a cave wall."

The **location is part of the text**.

Even better, the same Singing Mountain place-name appears in murals back at Teotihuacan, suggesting people may have travelled to this particular landscape to perform rituals.

[Read the open-access Antiquity paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/painted-teotihuacan-glyphs-of-cave-19-chalcatzingo-morelos-mexico/536A5E711BB8DFED14BE189F4F1B9259)

## FOUND OBJECT · “revelling in cultural irrelevance since 1987”

Yesterday Kevin J. O'Conner quietly released *Parataxis* through his tiny Bremerton imprint Tinty Music: five pieces of abstract electronic improvisation recorded and edited during September, made with VCV Rack.

That's fine. What got me was the label's description:

**“Revelling in cultural irrelevance since 1987.”**

There is an entire DIY philosophy hiding in that.

Not obscurity as failed promotion. **Obscurity as permission.**

Make the record because the investigation itself warrants making the record. Release it because finished objects are useful evidence that the investigation happened.

Also, unusually, the individual pieces become CC BY-SA 4.0 after purchase, so the artifact is explicitly allowed to keep mutating.

## STUDIO FUEL · stop treating paper as the neutral thing underneath the art

An exhibition marking 50 years of Brooklyn's Dieu Donné papermill is built from more than 110 works by 80 artists. The techniques are the useful bit: **pulp painting, paper casting, stenciling, and line and chiaroscuro watermarking**.

In other words, the sheet itself can contain the image before anybody draws, prints or collages onto it.

That's an excellent inversion for notebook-cover brain.

Instead of:

**paper → receive image**

try:

**paper → become image**

You don't need a papermill to steal the mechanism. Laminate translucent scraps between sheets. Cut windows before adding imagery. Embed thread. Distress only selected zones. Make a collage where the substrate establishes the composition before the first found image arrives.

The background stops being backstage.

[Pioneers in Hand Papermaking: 50 Years of Innovation at Dieu Donné](https://grolierclub.omeka.net/exhibits/show/dieudonne) · [Dieu Donné's 50th anniversary](https://www.dieudonne.org/50th-anniversary)

## RABBIT HOLE · history where both sides may be wrong

A recent free one-page solo RPG called *Epicedium* gives you a nasty little epistemological problem.

You're the final survivor of a lost civilization, awakened thousands of years later. Modern researchers desperately want you to explain the ruins they've been studying.

Except **you don't know why your civilization collapsed either**.

So play becomes an exchange between two incomplete models of the same past. Archaeologists have physical evidence without lived context. You have lived context without knowing what happened after your own experience ended. Either side can misinterpret things.

That's much more interesting than the standard oracle model where a roll reveals hidden canonical truth.

There may be **no privileged camera**.

For solo play, that suggests a useful oracle design: generate two incompatible explanations and never roll to determine which is objectively correct. Future evidence can strengthen or damage either model, but the game doesn't owe you an answer.

That is basically historiography converted into a mechanic.

## CROSSWIRE · Cave 19 × Meat Cathedral = architecture that conjugates verbs

Cave 19 gives us something better than environmental storytelling.

The glyph isn't simply lore attached to architecture. The shelf, soot, cliff, glyph and journey required to reach the place jointly produce meaning.

Now apply that to a fictional religious structure.

A chapel shouldn't merely contain text saying **CONFESSION**.

Its architecture could make confession physically possible.

A reliquary room might only be readable clockwise because each station changes the interpretation of the next. A ledger chamber could require two people standing in different places to see complementary portions of an inscription. A staircase might progressively conceal earlier images as later ones become visible.

The building doesn't house the ritual.

**The building is grammatical machinery for performing the ritual.**

That seems substantially more fertile than another dungeon room with an inscription conveniently explaining what happened there.

## 20 MINUTES · make an archaeological lie

Take one index card or notebook page.

Invent **one artifact** from a society that doesn't exist: receipt, devotional card, ration token, maintenance ticket, transit stub, warranty label, prayer strip, inspection stamp, whatever.

Do not explain the society.

Then write three interpretations of the artifact:

1. what its maker believed it meant;
2. what a historian 600 years later thinks it meant;
3. what physical evidence on the object contradicts **both** of them.

Ten minutes making the thing. Ten minutes arguing with it.

Stop there.

You haven't made lore. You've made **evidence with a problem inside it**.

### today's residue

There's a useful distinction running through today's pile:

**an artifact can store information its maker never intended to record.**

A cave inscription accidentally preserves geography and ritual movement. Handmade paper carries decisions before ink arrives. A lost civilization leaves objects that disagree with memory. A tiny electronic release documents somebody's private investigation whether or not culture notices.

Objects leak.

For writing, games, notebooks and collage, that leakage may be more interesting than whatever you deliberately put on the front.
