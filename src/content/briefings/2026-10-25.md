# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**October 25, 2026 · No. 026**

*A band once drew record sleeves in a van. A museum preserved mole feet for toothache. A print can have a ghost, and a number sequence can write its own instructions.*

Today's question: **what survives when an object passes through somebody else's hands?**

## THE SIGNAL · nothing worth forcing

**No major signal today.** The most interesting recent music story is about a band discovering that its old master tape contains more than its own members remembered. That's not breaking news in the usual sense. It's better.

## LISTENING ROOM · P.E.E. made the record sleeves in the van

On **October 23**, Jude Noel's [Bandcamp Daily interview with P.E.E.](https://daily.bandcamp.com/features/pee-now-more-charm-and-more-tender-feature) revisited the San Francisco band's awkward, gloriously scrappy 1990s guitar music. The quartet reunited in 2026 for the thirtieth anniversary of *Now, More Charm and More Tender*, remastered and reissued this year.

The band's first seven-inch was self-pressed. Jackets were photocopied, and individual covers were drawn in crayon during long drives between shows. When they couldn't afford merchandise, they thrifted shirts and screen-printed them on the road.

There's a particularly good archival twist: the group found the original master tape of its murkily mixed second album, *The Roaring Mechanism*. Singer/guitarist Kelly Green says the band had trouble even making out its own lyrics on the released version. The recovered tape may finally let them hear what they actually recorded.

The music is all twitchy guitar angles, unexpected turns, melody, and a rhythm section that seems to be arguing with the architecture. Green's description of the band's division of labor is memorable: Jim Stanley builds a strange house with guitar, Andee Connors attacks it with drums, and the others try to hold it upright.

**The discovery isn't that imperfect DIY artifacts were secretly perfect. It's that their imperfections became part of how people encountered them, while the underlying work kept waiting to be heard.**

Start with ["IHOP" or "Plankton" on the band's Bandcamp](https://peemusic.bandcamp.com/album/now-more-charm-and-more-tender). This is a rediscovery, not a claim that the band has released a new studio album.

## FOUND OBJECT · eleven things people carried for toothache

[Wellcome Collection's 1937 photograph of eleven English dental charms](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/cu69wep6) is a tiny museum of intensely specific human hope.

The objects include two mole's feet carried in a pocket in Norfolk, a bag containing a crust of bread, teeth kept in a case, stones associated with toothache, and a bag of pig's teeth tied around an infant's neck for teething. There is also orris root, historically used as a teething aid.

These are **historical folk remedies, not effective medical treatments**. The photograph was made in November 1937; the objects themselves came from earlier collecting and beliefs.

What's fascinating is their physical grammar. A tooth for a tooth. A thing carried against the body. An ordinary scrap made important by the story attached to it. Folklorist Edward Lovett collected such objects, sometimes explaining charms through symbolic resemblance. [Wellcome's account of his collecting](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/XN0r-BAAALrGoDf0) is worth reading alongside the catalog record.

The photograph is not merely evidence of mistaken medicine. It's evidence that people gave portable objects jobs that their material properties could not perform.

**An amulet is an instruction disguised as a thing: carry this, keep it close, believe this connection matters.**

## STUDIO FUEL · pull the ghost print, not just the print

A *monotype* is made by painting or inking a smooth surface, then transferring the image to paper. Unlike an engraved plate meant to reproduce an edition, the painted arrangement changes or disappears in the transfer.

But don't clean the plate after the first pull. Press another sheet against it.

The weaker second impression is called a **ghost print**. It preserves the residue the first sheet didn't take. Sometimes the incomplete, broken version is more interesting than the original.

[Grinnell College Museum of Art's practical guide](https://www.grinnell.edu/campus-life/arts-culture/museum/educational-resources/monotypes) demonstrates this with simple materials, including plastic wrap, paper, paint, and a spoon. The [Cleveland Museum of Art's discussion](https://www.clevelandart.org/articles/unique-prints) explains why the transfer is inherently unpredictable.

Here's the stealable mechanism: **don't treat the second impression as a failed copy. Give it a different purpose.**

Use the first as a landscape, the second as a recollection of that landscape. The first is an official portrait; the second is how the subject appears in a dream. Or use the ghost as the only surviving image of something that has disappeared.

You don't need a printing press. You need a smooth surface, a little paint, two sheets, and the willingness to let the transfer make decisions.

## RABBIT HOLE · the sequence that reads its own handwriting

Consider these digits:

`1 2 2 1 1 2 1 2 2 1 2 2 1 1 2 ...`

Now divide them wherever the digit changes:

`1 | 22 | 11 | 2 | 1 | 22 | 1 | 22 | 11 | 2 | 22 | 11 | ...`

Count the lengths of those groups:

`1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2 ...`

**You get the original sequence back.**

This is the *Kolakoski sequence*, a self-describing string of ones and twos. Its run lengths reproduce its own digits. It's an established mathematical object, discussed as early as 1939, not a new claimed discovery.

Here's the part that makes it a real rabbit hole: mathematicians still have not proved that ones and twos occur equally often in the long run, even though calculations strongly suggest that they do. The easy-looking question remains open.

The [Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences, entry A000002](https://oeis.org/A000002), and [Wolfram MathWorld's explanation](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/KolakoskiSequence.html) provide the formal details.

The creative implication is deliciously strange. A pattern doesn't need an external score if its contents also encode its structure. You could turn 1 into a short guitar scrape and 2 into a long one, then hear what happens when the score becomes a performance.

## CROSSWIRE · a ghost print as a folk charm

Put Wellcome's dental amulets beside the ghost print.

One tradition assigns power to an object through *resemblance or contact*. The other physically transfers pigment through contact, leaving a fainter second image.

Now imagine a fictional practice in which a charm is printed twice.

The **first pull** is given to the person who needs protection. The **ghost pull** is kept by the maker. When the charm is used, a mark appears on the ghost. When the ghost fades entirely, the protection ends.

You have a compact supernatural rule, but also a social arrangement. The maker can monitor a distant person's condition. The protected person must trust somebody who retains the other half of the artifact. Stealing the ghost might be more dangerous than stealing the charm.

That's an immediately usable solo-RPG mechanism: two linked objects, one action that affects both, and a relationship defined by who possesses which copy.

**The interesting collision isn't just visual. It's the idea that making a copy creates an obligation.**

## 20 MINUTES · print a charm with an inconvenient warranty

Take a piece of plastic wrap or foil, a little washable paint, two scraps of paper, and a spoon.

Paint a crude symbol for an invented protection: against forgetting names, losing your way in familiar buildings, or hearing a bell that nobody else hears.

Press the first paper onto the painted surface. Lift it. Without adding paint, press the second sheet to make its ghost.

Give the first print to an imaginary traveler. Keep the second in the archive.

On the back of the ghost, write three sentences: what the charm protects against; what visible change in the ghost proves it has been used; and what the maker can demand from the traveler because they hold the second copy.

Stop there. Two prints, three sentences, one very questionable little bargain.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A band's old master tape contains a clearer version of its past. A pocket charm turns an object into a promise. A second print preserves what the first left behind. A sequence uses its own digits to describe its shape.

**A copy isn't always a lesser original. Sometimes it's the part that explains what the original was doing.**

And sometimes it's a bag of pig's teeth with an extraordinarily ambitious job description.
