# Daily Side-Channel — November 18, 2026

*The Leaf That Kept Its Skeleton*

A Finnish band puts an extreme-metal voice over almost indecently shiny dance music. Victorian makers reduce leaves to delicate vascular ghosts. A solo game sends you beneath an abandoned mining town with memories that won't hold still.

Today's question: **what remains recognizable when you remove the thing that normally defines it?**

## THE SIGNAL — no major signal today

No major signal worth forcing. But **Pharaoh Overlord's _7_**, reviewed by JR Moores for Bandcamp Daily on **November 17, 2026**, is a genuinely worthwhile recent listen. Members of the Circle orbit have moved far from their stoner-rock origins: gleaming synths, space-rock repetition, dance textures, and Aaron Turner's ferocious vocals coexist without smoothing each other out. Richard Dawson appears on "Arms Of None"; "Under Distant Sun" wanders through several electronic idioms.

The trick isn't novelty-by-collision. It's refusing the assumption that heavy vocals require heavy instrumentation. Try "Arms Of None" and listen to which element supplies the aggression: voice, rhythm, harmony, or merely your expectation.

## FOUND OBJECT — a bouquet of botanical ghosts

In 1864, two books described the production of **"phantom flowers"**: leaves and seed structures reduced to their intricate veins. The process involved maceration, removing softer tissue, and sometimes bleaching. These weren't simply botanical diagrams. They were fragile, physical remnants of actual plants, displayed as pale bouquets.

The practice has older East Asian antecedents and became part of a nineteenth-century intersection of domestic craft, natural history, and preservation. The results resemble drawings that have somehow lost their paper.

The compelling object is the **network** left after most of the leaf disappears. It is simultaneously evidence of the original leaf and a new thing with its own visual character.

[Phantom Bouquets: Two Books on the Art of Skeleton Leaves (The Public Domain Review)](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/phantom-flowers/)

## STUDIO FUEL — subtract the recognizable part

A drawing exercise: sketch a household object, then erase everything except the lines that explain how its parts connect. For a stapler, preserve hinge, spring, and feed path. For a guitar, preserve strings, bridge, nut, and the path of vibration. Don't preserve its silhouette.

Make a second drawing using only that structural network. The new image needn't resemble the original. What matters is that its relationships remain traceable.

This is a way to invent visual forms without starting from a random blob. You're designing from **functional anatomy**, then allowing the anatomy to become ornamental.

## RABBIT HOLE — a memory can be a game resource

Raoul Schaupp's recent solo journaling game **_What Remains Below_** begins with torn diary pages and a return to an abandoned mining settlement. It uses a twelve-hour structure in three chapters: the approach, the village, and the mine. Memory, Courage, and Doubt affect the ending.

The interesting design decision is to make memory an active variable rather than a fixed backstory. What you discover below ground can change the meaning of what you thought you knew above it.

This is a game-page description, not a claim from a completed playthrough. The premise alone is worth examining as a structure for investigative solo play.

[What Remains Below (itch.io)](https://raoulschaupp.itch.io/what-remains-below)

## CROSSWIRE — the skeleton of a town

Combine skeleton leaves with the mine game's uncertain memories. Draw a settlement, then remove all buildings from the map. Preserve only the relationships that once supported life there: water channels, footpaths, shared walls, delivery routes, drainage, electrical connections.

Now give the returning character a memory of one building. The building is gone, but the network proves that something stood there. The problem is that **two different buildings could fit the same surviving connections**.

That creates a mystery without a hidden answer prepared in advance. Each new piece of evidence narrows the possibilities; a person who remembers the town may be confident and wrong.

The useful collision: archaeology as an exercise in reconstructing absent forms from surviving relationships, with memory supplying hypotheses rather than facts.

## 20 MINUTES — draw the thing that's missing

1. **5 minutes:** Draw a tiny settlement of six buildings and the paths, pipes, or wires connecting them.
2. **5 minutes:** Erase the buildings. Keep every connection and junction.
3. **5 minutes:** Write two incompatible explanations for what stood at the busiest junction.
4. **5 minutes:** Write a diary fragment from someone who insists one explanation is correct. Include one small physical detail that makes their certainty questionable.

Stop. You now have a map of absences and a witness who may be lying, mistaken, or remembering a different version of the place.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A voice remains unmistakably metal after its instrumental surroundings change. A leaf remains legible after its soft tissue is removed. A town persists as connections after its buildings disappear.

**Identity may survive subtraction, but it rarely survives unchanged.**
