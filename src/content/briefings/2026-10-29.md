## LISTENING ROOM · a childhood cassette gets swallowed by a rave

[Kill Alters — *We Can Rot Together*](https://killalters.bandcamp.com/album/we-can-rot-together) arrived October 16 on Hausu Mountain. Bonnie Baxter wrote and produced the album, with Nicos Kennedy handling mixing and mastering. The October 22 [Bandcamp Daily review](https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/kill-alters-we-can-rot-together-review) describes a record that behaves less like a conventional set of songs than an overstuffed, violently edited DJ set.

It lunges between digital hardcore, abrasive dance music, punk, and club sounds. Then, after a long stretch of electronic assault, it drops a karaoke recording of "Toy Soldiers" made in **1993**. Not a nostalgic cover recorded to sound old: an actual fragment from Baxter's personal archive.

The album is interested in the mess of material already living in someone's head. Baxter reportedly built much of it using the Patterning drum-machine app, linking tracks by small musical ideas rather than tidy genre boundaries.

**Stealable idea:** don't insert an old recording merely as atmosphere. Put it somewhere that changes the listener's understanding of the surrounding music. A childhood tape in the middle of a hostile rave isn't a break from the work; it can be the reason the work feels hostile.

## FOUND OBJECT · a book whose plot has hinges

The British Library acquired a rare copy of [*Mother Shipton* (1771)](https://scroll.in/article/1035731/what-did-lift-the-flap-books-look-like-in-the-18th-century), a **harlequinade** or *turn-up book*. It was made from engraved sheets folded into an accordion, with upper and lower flaps that reveal different halves of pictures and verses as they're lifted.

This isn't a modern pop-up pasted onto an old story. **The physical manipulation is how the story advances.** The verses instruct the reader when to turn the paper. Harlequin, Columbine, and the legendary soothsayer Mother Shipton move through scenes that are incomplete until the reader operates them.

The surviving copy was repaired in **1783**, according to an ownership inscription. That's almost the perfect detail: a cheap interactive entertainment was used hard enough that somebody bothered to mend its moving parts. The British Library's account also notes troubling racist imagery in the historical illustrations.

The object asks a useful design question: what information can exist *only because the reader changed the page*? Not a secret hidden under a flap, necessarily. A sentence whose subject changes when the upper half of the picture is replaced.

[Read Maddy Smith's British Library account, republished by Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1035731/what-did-lift-the-flap-books-look-like-in-the-18th-century)

## STUDIO FUEL · the alphabet made of spare parts

On **October 27**, Letterform Archive launched a [preview site and interactive composer for *Super-Veloz*](https://super-veloz.com/), alongside an [interview with Alex Trochut and Andreu Balius](https://letterformarchive.org/news/super-veloz-q-and-a/).

Joan Trochut's Spanish **Super-Veloz** system, released in the 1940s, was metal type broken into modular components. Printers could combine the parts into letters, ornaments, and illustrations. A finite collection of shapes generated a huge variety of outcomes without requiring the printer to cut a new image for every job.

The site also includes a rediscovered **1979 recording of Joan Trochut** discussing his life. His grandson Alex had never heard his voice. The book itself is forthcoming; the online composer is the thing to try now.

What to steal: **make the alphabet and the illustrations from the same parts**. Invent four shapes (a hook, wedge, circle, and bar). Make a wordmark, a little animal, and a warning sign using only those shapes. The visual unity emerges from the shared vocabulary rather than from applying a template afterward.

[Try the composer](https://super-veloz.com/) · [Read the October 27 interview](https://letterformarchive.org/news/super-veloz-q-and-a/)

## RABBIT HOLE · the tiny flower that can expose a fraudulent book

The University of Birmingham's [Compositor](https://compositor.bham.ac.uk/) is a searchable database of **eighteenth-century printers' ornaments**: little floral decorations, tailpieces, woodcuts, cast ornaments, and *fleurons* that decorate otherwise ordinary pages.

Why catalog tiny decorations?

Because a printer could reuse the same physical ornament across different jobs. An anonymous pamphlet and a known book might contain a matching block, potentially helping researchers identify who printed the anonymous work. The project's researchers explicitly note uses in investigating **piracy and fraud**. A match is a clue, not automatic proof: printing materials could change hands.

This is book history turning into detective work. The ornament wasn't intended to be a signature. It was decoration. But the thing a printer repeated without thinking can become more revealing than the words deliberately placed above it.

The site supports browsing by printer, city, date, and author, plus visual search. Even without doing bibliographical detective work, it's a wonderful cabinet of tiny black-and-white monsters, flowers, borders, and impossible furniture.

[Browse Compositor](https://compositor.bham.ac.uk/) · [Read about its forensic purpose](https://compositor.bham.ac.uk/about/)

## CROSSWIRE · the forbidden printer's mark

Combine the harlequinade's moving page with Compositor's forensic ornaments.

In a fictional city, every official announcement carries a tiny **moth-shaped printer's ornament**. A prohibited pamphlet also carries the moth, but one wing is damaged. The investigator finds an older government notice with exactly the same damaged wing.

Now introduce a folded broadside whose headline changes when the top flap is lifted. The official version names a criminal. The other version names the institution that printed the accusation.

The adventure isn't about rolling to find a hidden document. It's about learning to **read production evidence**: worn blocks, reused ornaments, repairs, folds, and the physical sequence of reading. Who owned the printing equipment? Who could alter the pages? Why did someone keep using the damaged moth?

The collision suggests a solo-RPG rule: **every document must contain one detail its author didn't intend as a message**. That detail can become a clue later. A fantasy investigation suddenly has an information system rather than a pile of exposition.

## 20 MINUTES · print a lie with a recognizable defect

Take one sheet of paper, a pen, and scissors if you want them.

1. **Five minutes:** invent a printer's mark small enough to fit in a corner. Draw it three times. Give the third copy one distinctive defect: a broken wing, missing tooth, or crooked ray.
2. **Seven minutes:** write two contradictory notices from a fictional town. One says a place is safe; the other says nobody returns from it. Put the *same defective mark* beneath both.
3. **Five minutes:** fold a flap over the first notice so that lifting it changes the accusation or the name of the authority. Don't add a paragraph explaining the trick.
4. **Three minutes:** write an investigator's marginal note describing what the matching defect proves, and what it *doesn't* prove.

Stop. You've made a prop, a contradiction, and a playable lead. No lore encyclopedia, no special software, no procurement of a nineteenth-century printing press.
