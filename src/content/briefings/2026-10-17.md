# Daily Side-Channel
*October 17, 2026 · No. 018*

*Twenty-copy albums, a calendar the size of a small table, and the discovery that a spinning wheel can literally manufacture a musical note.*

Today's question: **what changes when a tool becomes a shared language?**

## THE SIGNAL · no major signal today

No major signal today. There's a very good archival release from yesterday, but calling it a cultural earthquake would be ridiculous. It's enough that ten recordings have escaped obscurity.

## FOUND OBJECT · a city pop scene that barely made it onto tape

Yesterday, October 16, Habibi Funk released [*Habibi Funk 035: Beirut City Pop*](https://habibifunkrecords.bandcamp.com/album/habibi-funk-035-beirut-city-pop): ten tracks from Lebanon's private-press recordings of the late 1970s and 1980s.

The music wanders through disco, soul, jazz, bossa, rock, and soft-focus pop. Start with Stephanie Stephanou's *You Turn Me On*, then Games' *Someday*.

The remarkable thing is the scale of the original circulation. According to the label, Andromeda's album may have existed in **twenty copies or fewer**. Some tracks were never officially issued at all; they survived as tapes exchanged between musicians.

Many players belonged to a loose circle around Ziad Rahbani's Bypass Studio. Others recorded elsewhere, but musicians and ideas traveled between projects. The compilation's interviews and photographs help reconstruct those connections.

This isn't a story about a genre suddenly appearing. It's about a musical community that already existed, but whose evidence was scattered among its participants.

**A scene isn't always a record-store shelf. Sometimes it's twenty cassettes and the people who know whom to call.**

## STUDIO FUEL · build a dungeon that fits inside one die

[Notebook RPG](https://aldinthemage.itch.io/notebook-rpg) by AldinTheMage is a free, open-source solo dungeon crawler designed for a pocket notebook, pencil, and **one d6**.

Its pocket booklet was updated in February 2026. The author also provides source files and publishes the game under GPLv3.

The clever constraint isn't the dungeon. It's refusing to require more than six possible outcomes at any decision point. That keeps the whole machine portable.

**Treat your smallest available randomizer as a design budget.** Six results can be six room types, six complications, or six ways a conversation turns sour. If you need thirty-six outcomes, roll twice, but make sure the extra complexity earns its space.

One recent player comment on the itch page reports that combat becomes too easy with some starting builds. That's anecdotal playtest feedback, not a settled verdict. Approach this first as a compact design to study and tinker with, rather than a finely balanced tactical campaign.

## RABBIT HOLE · the monastery had a giant paper computer

Around 1455, the Benedictine community at San Zeno in Verona had a remarkable timekeeping device: a **three-layer rotating calendar nearly 1.3 metres across**.

The San Zeno Wheel was made from pine covered with vellum. Its large disk tracked dates, saints' days, and changing lengths of daylight. Smaller disks could be turned to work out lunar phases, important for calculating movable feasts such as Easter.

This is a *volvelle*: an information instrument that computes by physically aligning rotating parts. Think of a slide rule recruited into monastic life and enlarged until several people can consult it together.

The fascinating detail is its social scale. It wasn't just a clever personal gadget. It helped a community coordinate the rhythm of days and seasons.

The machine didn't tell people what to believe. It helped them agree on **when** to do things.

[Sarah Griffin's research in the Oxford University archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A2cc0b5d0-81d1-469b-8244-8daca7d8f716) identifies it as the only surviving device of its type and reconstructs its use.

A fantasy monastery with a mysterious prophetic clock is fine. A monastery that needs someone to maintain a giant wooden spreadsheet so breakfast and Easter happen on the correct days is, frankly, better.

## SOUND CABINET · the machine that turns teeth into pitch

The [Science Museum's Savart wheel](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co5983/savarts-wheel) is a beautiful piece of nineteenth-century acoustic apparatus: **eight toothed brass wheels on a common spindle**, made by William Ladd in London in 1870.

Hold a card against the teeth and rotate the wheels. Slowly, you hear separate clicks. Faster, the clicks fuse into a tone. Different tooth counts produce different pitches at the same rotation speed.

The underlying idea is wonderfully plain:

**Frequency = number of teeth × revolutions per second.**

A hypothetical forty-tooth wheel spinning three times per second gives 120 impacts per second, or a 120 Hz fundamental repetition rate.

Robert Hooke had demonstrated related toothed-wheel sound experiments in 1681. Félix Savart later used such devices to investigate hearing.

The mechanism exposes something that electric guitars and synthesizers can make easy to forget: a musical pitch is a sufficiently fast, sufficiently regular event.

**Sound is arithmetic with air attached.**

## CROSSWIRE · a calendar wheel and a noise wheel walk into a monastery

Put San Zeno's volvelle beside Savart's acoustic wheel.

One translates rotation into **a readable date**. The other translates rotation into **an audible frequency**. Both turn invisible cycles into something people can perceive and use.

Now steal the mechanism for a solo RPG.

Imagine a community whose calendar is maintained by an enormous wheel that sounds a different note for each season. Nobody reads the date; they listen for it.

An incorrect note might mean a broken tooth, a misaligned disk, or a deliberate alteration.

Suddenly a mystery has three layers: a mechanical fault, a communal ritual, and a possible conspiracy. You don't need supernatural intervention. The apparatus is already capable of making everyone live by the wrong day.

**The design principle:** make one piece of infrastructure responsible for both practical coordination and cultural meaning. When it fails, the consequences spread beyond the machine.

## 20 MINUTES · invent a clock that can lie

Draw a circle and divide it into six wedges, one for each sound:

1. Bell
2. Scrape
3. Knock
4. Whistle
5. Hum
6. Silence

Roll a d6 twice. The first result is what the town **expects**; the second is what it **hears**.

If the results match, write down **who is disappointed**. If they're different, write down **who benefits from the mistake**.

Then answer three questions in one sentence each: Who maintains the clock? What ordinary activity depends on it? What evidence would distinguish sabotage from wear?

Sketch the clock. Stop. You have a playable mystery seed, not another elaborate project you need to finish.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A cassette can hold a scene together. A single die can support a whole dungeon. A rotating calendar can synchronize a monastery. A toothed wheel can turn arithmetic into noise.

**Tools don't just help people do things. Sometimes they teach people how to organize their world.**

And sometimes somebody files down one tooth, and suddenly it's the wrong damn Tuesday.
