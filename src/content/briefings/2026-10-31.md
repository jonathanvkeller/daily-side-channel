# DAILY SIDE-CHANNEL
**October 31, 2026 · No. 032**

*The Advantage of Going Second*

A label that has spent nearly fifty years keeping difficult music available, a debt that once came in two matching pieces of wood, and three dice with no best die.

Today's question: **what happens when the evidence is trustworthy but the rules aren't?**

---

## THE SIGNAL · nothing worth forcing

**No major signal today.** An October 28 guide to an extraordinary independent record label is the recent thing worth your attention. No emergency attached.

## LISTENING ROOM · the label that built a home for the unclassifiable

On **October 28**, Bandcamp Daily published Erick Bradshaw's [guide to ReR Megacorp](https://daily.bandcamp.com/label-profile/rer-megacorp-label-profile), the experimental-music label Chris Cutler began as Recommended Records in 1978.

The catalog is a glorious refusal to decide whether music should be rock, free improvisation, composition, tape experiment, or a piece of machinery having a nervous breakdown. The label's **ReR Quarterly** series even paired records with magazines. Distribution and documentation were part of the same object.

One particularly good doorway is **ZGA**, a group founded in Riga in 1984. Unable to rehearse acoustically without disturbing neighbors, its members developed homemade instruments from scrap, springs, and shaped metal, amplified with contact microphones. They called these constructions *zgamoniums*. The results aren't merely industrial clatter: the objects make pitched, rhythmic, unexpectedly articulate sounds. [Soundohm's account of the instruments](https://www.soundohm.com/product/zgamoniums-cd) adds the wonderfully practical origin story.

Try ZGA's **“There Is Only Wind On Top Of The Mountain”** through the Bandcamp guide. The useful discovery is the label itself: an institution that kept a path open for sounds that ordinary genre bins would have stranded.

## FOUND OBJECT · a debt that can be physically reunited

The [Science Museum Group preserves English Exchequer tally sticks from around 1440](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co60506/medieval-exchequer-tally-sticks). They're small lengths of notched wood used in recording financial obligations.

Here's the clever part. A tally could be **split lengthwise after the notches were cut**, leaving two parties with matching pieces. Bring them back together and the grain and original notches help authenticate the record. An added notch would not match both halves.

The [Bank of England displays a 1694 tally](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/museum/whats-on/exhibitions/325-years-exhibition/payments-through-time) documenting one of its earliest loans to the government. These weren't just quaint predecessors of spreadsheets. They were physical records designed for people who did not have to trust one another's copy.

Many museum survivors are only one half, called a *stock*. Somewhere, once, there was a counterpart.

**The information wasn't only in the marks. It was in the relationship between two objects.**

## STUDIO FUEL · make an original with a copying machine

Italian designer **Bruno Munari** began experimenting with photocopiers in the 1960s. His *Xerografie originali* were not ordinary duplicates: he moved images, marks, and materials across the copier glass **during the scan**, stretching and smearing them into compositions that could not be reproduced exactly.

The [Centro Pecci's account of a 1970 work](https://centropecci.it/il-centro-pecci/collezione/opere/xerografia-originale/) describes a motorcycle image dragged into an elongated blur. [Centre Pompidou holds later examples](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/ressources/oeuvre/cjE9aXA). The apparatus built to preserve an image faithfully became a way to deform it deliberately.

**Steal the time-based rule:** start with one plain mark, then let motion during capture determine its final form. A slow-shutter phone photograph of a moving paper shape can explore the same principle without needing a vintage copier.

The interesting question is not how to make a better copy. It's how to make a machine's normal operation produce something it was never designed to make.

## RABBIT HOLE · three dice, no champion

These are **nontransitive dice**, a well-established recreational-math result. Imagine three six-sided dice with these faces:

| Die | Six faces |
| --- | --- |
| A | 2, 2, 4, 4, 9, 9 |
| B | 1, 1, 6, 6, 8, 8 |
| C | 3, 3, 5, 5, 7, 7 |

Roll two different dice. Higher number wins.

**A beats B more often than not. B beats C more often than not. And C beats A more often than not.**

Each advantage is exactly **20 out of 36 possible rolls**, or **5/9 (55.6%)**. You can check the pairings yourself; there are no ties. The University of Cambridge's [NRICH activity](https://nrich.maths.org/content/id/7541/Non-transitive%20Dice.pdf) uses precisely these three dice.

This is not a contradiction. “Usually beats” isn't the same kind of relationship as “is larger than.” The probabilities can form a circle.

The nasty little game-design implication: **if one player chooses a die first, the second player can always choose a die with an advantage**. There is no globally strongest option, only a favorable matchup.

## CROSSWIRE · perfect accounting, crooked procedure

Put the split tally beside the nontransitive dice.

Imagine a city where debts are recorded with impeccable wooden tallies. Nobody can quietly alter the amount owed. Every contract has a physical counterpart, and the halves fit.

But when a debt is disputed, the claimant must choose one of three ritual dice. The defendant chooses second.

The documents are honest. The procedure systematically favors the defendant.

That's a useful distinction for a fictional institution, mystery, or solo-RPG economy: **a system can be excellent at proving facts and terrible at deciding what to do with them**.

Now imagine three merchant houses, each associated with one die. They can all truthfully claim an advantage over another house, and none can claim supremacy over everyone. Alliances become more important than raw strength.

You haven't added a secret conspiracy. You've built an institution whose injustice is right there in the published rules.

## 20 MINUTES · settle a debt that nobody can settle fairly

Take a sheet of paper and one ordinary six-sided die.

**Five minutes:** invent three rival guilds. Assign them dice A, B, and C from the table above. Give each guild something the others need.

**Five minutes:** write a short contract between two guilds. Draw three notches across its edge, then tear the paper so each party holds a matching fragment. The fragments jointly authenticate the debt.

**Five minutes:** stage a dispute. The claimant chooses a die; the other party chooses a counter. To simulate a special die with an ordinary d6, map rolls 1–2 to its lowest distinct value, 3–4 to its middle distinct value, and 5–6 to its highest distinct value. Roll five contests.

**Five minutes:** write the ruling. Then write one sentence explaining why the losing side accepts, resists, or exploits a procedure everybody knows is tilted.

Stop. You have a contract, three factions, a probability engine, and a political problem without writing a single page of lore.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

A record label keeps difficult sounds circulating. A notched stick makes a debt verifiable by splitting it. A photocopier makes originals by failing to copy faithfully. Three dice make a circle out of apparent superiority.

**The rules of a system and the truth of its records are different things.**

Sometimes the books balance perfectly, and the game is still rigged.
