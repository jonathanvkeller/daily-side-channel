# Daily Side-Channel
**November 1, 2026 · No. 033**

*The Wind Doesn't Know the Song*

A rock record without guitars gets its listening party in a car meet. A medieval almanac unfolds from someone's belt. And a musical instrument waits for the weather to decide what it sounds like.

**Today's question: what happens when you build something that someone else, or something else, has to play?**

## THE SIGNAL: no major signal today

**No major signal today.** The recent discovery worth keeping is a record whose launch was designed around the sound systems that would play it, rather than a press-release event.

## LISTENING ROOM: twenty cars, zero guitars

London quartet **RIP Magic** released their debut album, *1,2,3*, on **October 30, 2026**, via section1. They call it a rock record without guitars: live bass and drums, synths, samples, and vocals pull from dub, dance music, and hip-hop.

The particularly good detail is the listening party. According to an October 30 report in *Still Listening*, the band teamed up with South London car-meet crew Southeast Tunerz; around twenty cars played the album through their own sound systems. An album launch became a distributed, bass-heavy listening apparatus.

That's more interesting than the familiar claim that an album defies genre. They removed the instrument that traditionally announces *rock*, then let bass, percussion, and playback hardware do the heavy lifting. Different cars almost certainly colored the sound differently; the reported fact is the multi-car playback, not a documented comparison of their acoustics.

Start with **"Screwdark"**, then try **"Stadium Drive"**. The record's changing environments are the point, not a hidden guitar waiting to enter.

## FOUND OBJECT: the calendar that hung from a belt

**Wellcome Collection's MS.40** is a late-fifteenth-century folding almanac. Its vellum sheets carry calendars, planetary and eclipse tables, and diagrams traditionally called the Vein Man and Zodiac Man. The cover bears an early date of **1463**.

Folded, the manuscript is roughly **14 by 5 centimeters**. The original vellum cover even has a tag so it could be suspended from a cord at the girdle.

Think about the interface. You don't open a large volume on a desk. You take a little object from your belt and unfold a much larger reference surface, almost like a paper map. Astronomy, calendrical calculation, and historical medical astrology are compressed into something portable. Those medical ideas belong to the history of belief and practice, not to modern care.

The surprising thing isn't simply that medieval people made pocket reference tools. It's that **the folded object and the expanded information surface were deliberately different sizes**. The book's body changed to match the job.

[Examine MS.40 at Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/sq4rjv47) · [See another fifteenth-century folding almanac at the Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/manuscript/77000)

## STUDIO FUEL: play the GM, let the character surprise you

Cezar Capacle's **Triple-O: The Player Character Emulator** reverses a familiar solo-RPG arrangement. Instead of playing a character while an oracle emulates the GM, you can run the world as GM and let the procedure emulate what a player character chooses to do.

Its central question is wonderfully modest: what would this character *obviously* do, and what might divert them? Capacle calls the three possibilities **Obvious, Option, and Odd**. Traits, moods, and pressures help determine which action emerges.

The expanded 52-page zine provides examples and tables; its listed digital price is **$11.90**. You don't need to buy it to borrow the underlying design lesson: **make characters unpredictable through pressures on their established personalities, not through random nonsense**.

A cautious scavenger might normally check the door. A plausible alternative is to bargain with whoever is inside. An odd action becomes interesting only if something about this particular scavenger makes it possible.

This could be especially useful when solo-playing a published adventure you've already read. You know the dungeon. You don't have to know what the person exploring it will decide.

[Explore Triple-O and its examples](https://capacle.itch.io/triple-o-zine)

## RABBIT HOLE: wind doesn't pluck the string

The Smithsonian preserves a mid-1960s **Aeolian harp** built by Dennis Durogi. Its maker explained that the strings could all be tuned to the same note, while the wind would excite different overtones depending on string thickness, angle, and pressure.

Why does a string sing when nobody touches it?

Air flowing past a wire can shed little vortices alternately from either side. Those repeated changes in force can push the wire into vibration. If the forcing lines up with a natural vibration of the string, it can produce an audible tone. This is **vortex shedding**, the same general phenomenon behind some humming wires and even visibly bouncing frost-covered lines.

It's established fluid mechanics, not mystical wind music. The University of Alaska Fairbanks has a remarkably clear explanation, and Worcester Polytechnic Institute documents an experimental harp placed in a wind tunnel.

The weird part: **the instrument has a stable physical identity, but the weather selects which parts of that identity become audible**.

[See the Smithsonian harp and its maker's letter](https://www.si.edu/object/nmah_608142) · [Read the physics at the University of Alaska Fairbanks](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/aeolian-harp) · [Hear about the WPI wind-tunnel experiment](https://wp.wpi.edu/musicalmachines/2021/05/15/aeolian-harp-project/)

## CROSSWIRE: a character is a wind harp

Put Triple-O beside the Aeolian harp.

A badly designed random NPC does arbitrary things. A rigidly scripted NPC always does the same thing. But a wind harp has a third possibility: **a fixed set of strings, activated differently by changing conditions**.

Give a character three permanent tensions: a promise they won't break, a fear they hide, and a person they need. These are the strings. Then apply weather: hunger, humiliation, unexpected kindness, a public accusation.

You don't need a personality-changing die roll. You need to ask which tension the situation excites.

The result can surprise you while remaining recognizably *that character*. The same pressure might make another character sing a completely different note.

For a solo campaign, this gives supporting characters continuity without making them puppets. For writing, it provides a way to produce a turn in a scene that feels discovered rather than imposed.

## 20 MINUTES: three kinds of weather

Take an index card. Invent a keeper of an abandoned relay station.

1. **Five minutes:** write three permanent facts: what the keeper promised, what they fear, and whom they miss.
2. **Five minutes:** introduce a traveler who asks to use the transmitter. Write the obvious response, one plausible alternative, and one strange but character-consistent response.
3. **Five minutes:** roll a d6: 1–3 selects obvious, 4–5 selects alternative, 6 selects strange. Write what happens in three sentences. This is a tiny exercise rule, not Triple-O's official resolution procedure.
4. **Five minutes:** change just one environmental pressure: a storm arrives, the traveler knows a forbidden name, or the transmitter begins receiving the keeper's own voice. Repeat the choices. Notice which permanent fact starts to resonate.

Stop there. You have one character who behaves consistently without behaving predictably.
