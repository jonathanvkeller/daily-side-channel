# Daily Side-Channel
**October 26, 2026 · No. 027**

*A band that never existed has a newly reviewed record. A book wants to swallow its reader. And in 1639, somebody put an encrypted argument about love and luck on a handheld fan.*

Today's question: **what if understanding an object means doing something to it?**

## THE SIGNAL · no major signal

**No major signal today.** There is, however, a new review of a genuinely odd musical construction. That's worth a listen, not a breaking-news siren.

## LISTENING ROOM · the garage-sale tapes are imaginary; the music isn't

In a review published **October 26**, Marat Ingeldeev examines composer **Oliver Leith's _Doom and the Dooms_**. The premise is an invented archival discovery: Leith supposedly bought damaged tapes at a jumble sale and transcribed the songs of a forgotten band that played a Bonfire Night gig. **That band's history is fiction**, not a newly authenticated lost recording.

The actual music was commissioned by Wigmore Hall, premiered in June 2024, and performed for the recording by guitarist **Sean Shibe**, **GBSR Duo**, and **12 Ensemble**. It treats nine instrumental pieces as though they're recovered songs: distorted guitar figures, singing saw, strings, percussion, and little pockets of grandiose melancholy.

The useful trick is that Leith doesn't merely write a soundtrack *about* a fictional artifact. He makes the invented artifact the reason the music has this particular shape. The fictional tape is a compositional constraint, not an elaborate press-release costume.

Start with the review's descriptions of **“All I Ever Wanted”** (a two-note guitar idea gradually darkened by other instruments) and **“My Horse Named Dream”** (frantic distorted guitar).

[Read The Quietus review, October 26](https://thequietus.com/quietus-reviews/oliver-leith-doom-and-the-dooms-review/). The album's release was also announced on [October 19](https://pjharvey.net/news/oliver-leith-releases-doom-and-the-dooms/).

## FOUND OBJECT · a solo RPG that requires vandalism

**_SPINE_**, by **Backwards Tabletop**, is a solo book-game in which you inherit a strange volume assembled from essays, biographies, diary entries, and poetry about seekers of immortality. The scholarly apparatus is part of the fiction. So is the danger: reading the book gradually absorbs you.

You follow endnotes as prompts and track your increasing absorption on a clock. To resist the book's influence, **you must deface its text**. The game is meant to be played physically; its prompts can involve marking, drawing, and other interventions in the pages.

The creator says a single physical copy supports **three playthroughs**. Its damage is designed to survive more than one encounter. That's a lovely inversion of the usual RPG rulebook, which tries to remain a pristine authority: here, the rules are an object your character has to fight.

The game was recognized for innovation at the 2025 Indie Groundbreakers and nominated for a 2026 ENNIE, according to the creator. A digital/printable version is available for **$6.99**. Content notes include possession, loss of bodily control, and manipulative verbal child abuse.

[See the game, its mechanics, and content notes](https://backwards-tabletop.itch.io/spine).

## STUDIO FUEL · the field guide to waves that aren't all the same wave

In **1903**, Kyoto artist **Mori Yūzan** published **_Hamonshū_**, three volumes of wave and ripple designs. These were reference patterns for craftspeople, potentially used on lacquerware, sword fittings, and other decorative objects.

Open the scans and the astonishing thing is the variety. “Water” isn't one visual texture. It has wakes, curls, intersections, eddies, broken surfaces, and smooth bands. The book makes an apparently single subject into a catalog of distinct behaviors.

**Steal the classification method, not the drawings.** Choose something you normally represent with one symbol, such as smoke, rust, static, or a cracked wall. Draw six versions, each caused by a different physical condition. A worldbuilding sketch suddenly acquires weather, materials, and history without needing paragraphs of lore.

This is also an excellent way to build a consistent graphic vocabulary for a zine: one family of forms, many environmental explanations.

[Browse all three 1903 volumes and selected plates](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/hamonshu-a-japanese-book-of-wave-and-ripple-designs-1903).

## RABBIT HOLE · the handheld fan that argues in pictures

Around **1639**, Italian printmaker **Stefano della Bella** made a fan bearing **two rebus texts**: one about love, the other about fortune. A rebus swaps words or sounds for pictures, so reading becomes a little performance of interpretation.

This wasn't just a puzzle on paper. The surviving object at **The Metropolitan Museum of Art** is an etched, double-sided fan with a handle and fringe. You could cool your face while asking somebody to decode a proverb. A practical accessory was also a portable conversation trap.

The Met has transcribed and translated both sides. One of the love-side sentiments is the paradox that love is blind yet sees from far away. On the fortune side, the rebuses turn into sayings about luck and judgment.

The deeper rabbit hole is that rebus letters flourished as social objects: valentines, parlor amusements, messages to family. Their point wasn't secure encryption. It was making **the recipient perform the act of reading**.

[Inspect the fan and both translations](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/382852) · [Read The Met's essay on rebus letters](https://www.metmuseum.org/it/essays/love-is-blind-rebuses-in-the-met-collection).

## CROSSWIRE · make the reader the final component

The rebus fan asks its owner to *solve* the surface. _SPINE_ asks its owner to *damage* the surface. Leith's invented tape asks the listener to accept a fictional provenance as part of hearing real music.

These are three different ways an artifact can make a demand on its audience.

For a solo RPG, try a rule that doesn't say “roll to discover the truth.” Instead: **to discover the truth, alter the evidence**. Rotate a page, erase a word, fold a diagram, or replace a caption. The action reveals one interpretation while making another harder to recover.

That creates a more interesting decision than a standard investigation check. The character isn't choosing whether to learn something; they're choosing **which version of the record they are willing to destroy**.

The physical action should be safe and deliberate, of course. A pencil mark on a scrap of paper is plenty.

## 20 MINUTES · write a double-sided lie

Take one index card.

On the front, draw **three small symbols**: perhaps a key, a bell, and a river. Underneath, write one instruction that uses the drawings in place of nouns: “When the [bell] sounds, carry the [key] across the [river].”

On the back, write an alternate glossary. The bell is a person. The key is a promise. The river is a memory. Now read the front again using the back's meanings.

Finally, cross out **one** symbol on the front. Write one sentence explaining what knowledge the person holding the card has permanently lost.

That's it. A miniature artifact with two interpretations and one irreversible choice. No lore bible required.

### TODAY'S RESIDUE

An invented tape becomes a real performance. A rulebook becomes something you must resist. A wave becomes a whole taxonomy. A fan becomes a sentence you have to solve.

**Sometimes the missing piece of an artwork is the person holding it.**
