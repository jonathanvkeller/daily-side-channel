# Daily Side-Channel
**November 23, 2026 · The Type Specimen That Started Talking**

A printer's catalog accidentally writes nonsense poetry. A quiet national park refuses to explain its own clues. And a Victorian lathe makes images that look like photographs of mathematical orbits.

Today's question: **what happens when a demonstration becomes the artwork?**

## THE SIGNAL · Nothing worth forcing

**No major signal today.** Rather than recycle another November music roundup, today's issue follows three older and more durable discoveries.

## FOUND OBJECT · A type catalog with an accidental literary career

In 1874 the William H. Page company published *Specimens of Chromatic Wood Type, Borders, Etc.*, a lavish catalog demonstrating multicolored wood type. Its commercial purpose was to show printers what the type could do. But the sample phrases drift into involuntary surrealism. The Public Domain Review points out **“Geographical excursion knives home”** and **“Numerous stolen mind.”**

These aren't presented as deliberate avant-garde poems. That's what makes them interesting. Words chosen to demonstrate shapes and colors start producing meaning despite the catalog's intentions.

[Browse the specimen pages](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/specimens-of-chromatic-wood-type-and-borders-1874/).

**A specimen book can be a literary form when the examples are stranger than the instructions.**

## STUDIO FUEL · Write a specimen, not a poem

Make a one-page fake type specimen using four nonsense phrases of three to five words. Each phrase must demonstrate a visual property: width, weight, repetition, or collision. Don't try to make them profound. Set one in huge capitals, compress another into a narrow column, repeat a third until letters become texture, and let the fourth interrupt everything.

Only afterward read the page as a poem. Its meaning is a byproduct of typography. This works with hand lettering, cut paper, a printer, or a notebook cover.

**Design for letterforms first; interpret the language second.**

## RABBIT HOLE · Geometry made by a machine with a wobble

Edward J. Woolsey's *Specimens of Fancy Turning* (1869) contains thirty albumen photographs of geometric designs made on hand- or foot-powered lathes. Oval and eccentric chucks and an elliptical cutting frame produced interlocking curves resembling orbital diagrams.

[See the photographs and historical background](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/fancy-turning/).

Change how a lathe's rotation is offset or guided and a straightforward action becomes an elaborate pattern. These aren't new mathematical results. They're artifacts of mechanical geometry.

**The machine isn't making complexity by thinking harder. It's repeating a slightly displaced motion.**

## CROSSWIRE · A park that prints its own unreliable guidebook

*Perigon Falls*, a solo journaling RPG by Jarkaz Games, sends you through a national park with a map, changing weather, recovered ranger logs, and fragmentary observations. Its creator says there is no predetermined solution hidden in the rulebook. The story grows from connections the player makes, or declines to make.

[Explore Perigon Falls](https://jarkaz.itch.io/perigon-falls).

Now collide it with the 1874 type specimen. Every place in a fictional park has a visitor-center sign designed to demonstrate a different typeface. Its sample sentence is nonsense: “NUMEROUS STOLEN MIND,” “RIVER FORGETS ITS OFFICE,” “THE PATH IS EMPLOYED ELSEWHERE.”

Each sign identifies a real place and offers an apparently meaningless sentence the player might treat as evidence. The signs weren't intended as clues. Or perhaps the printer knew something.

**An accidental poem becomes an oracle only when somebody starts acting on it.**

This is an original scenario seed, not a mechanic from *Perigon Falls*.

## 20 MINUTES · The municipal type specimen

Draw a 3×3 grid. Invent a mundane public facility in each square: pumping station, picnic shelter, abandoned ticket booth, maintenance shed. Choose four squares and write an absurd three-to-five-word sign for each, in dramatically different lettering.

Draw a route through those four locations. Read the signs in route order as one statement. Write two sentences: what the traveler thinks it means, and what a bored municipal employee insists it means.

Stop there. You have a tiny setting, a graphic design, and an interpretation problem. No encyclopedia required.
